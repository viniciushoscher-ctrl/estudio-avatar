The doctor who has cared for every member of Congress since 2009 is out, CBS News and The Wall Street Journal reported on November 13, 2026.
He is Rear Admiral Brian Monahan of the U.S. Navy, the Attending Physician of the Capitol.
The same day, the Pentagon's inspector general released a report saying sleep aids and pain medication in his office were not properly tracked.
In the next minutes: what the report found, what Monahan loses, and how it ended for two other military doctors and commanders who fell before him.
Senate Majority Leader John Thune put it in six words: "My understanding is he's resigned," CBS News reported.
But not everyone tells it the same way.
The Wall Street Journal reported that Monahan stepped down. Punchbowl News reported that he was dismissed, The Daily Beast noted.
House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries said he had no knowledge of the circumstances, CBS reported.
Monahan himself left only a voicemail: "I am no longer serving with the Office of Attending Physicians," The Washington Post reported.
He and the Navy did not say whether he was fired or resigned, the Post reported. No successor has been announced.
So who exactly is the man who walked out of the Capitol?
Monahan has been the attending physician to Congress for about 17 years, through four presidents.
He is a two-star rear admiral, and he has also served as acting physician for the justices of the Supreme Court, The Daily Beast reported.
His office is old. The Navy has run medical care for Congress for 98 years, CBS reported.
It treats senators, House members, their staff, Supreme Court justices and even visitors to the Capitol.
According to the inspector general's report, the office has a staff of 5 physicians, 2 pharmacists and 11 Navy corpsmen, Military Times reported.
So what did the inspector general find inside that office?
The Pentagon inspector general found what it called a "gap in clinical quality management," CBS reported.
Some prescription drugs, including sleep aids and pain medication, were not properly inventoried or tracked, the report said.
According to Military Times, the office has had no outside oversight of its medical quality since 2013.
The watchdog warned that these gaps could "result in OAP beneficiaries unknowingly assuming health and safety risks."
In other words, members of Congress could be taking risks without knowing it.
How often were the controlled drugs actually checked?
From January 2023 to February 2026, the controlled-substance inventory was observed only 7 times, Military Times reported, citing the report.
That is seven checks in more than three years.
Investigators also found 5 inconsistencies in the medication records.
The report says the office does not allow Defense Department pharmacy inspections.
It made 5 recommendations, including an outside body to oversee the doctors, clear written rules for the staff, and a review of pharmacy inspection records.
And if the office is found to have broken federal law, the report says the Pentagon or the DEA could be asked to inspect the pharmacy.
But did Monahan help the investigators?
No. According to the report, Monahan refused to participate. He recused himself and sent investigators to Congress's lawyers, Military Times reported.
Then the House general counsel blocked the inspector general from internal documents, and blocked staff from talking to investigators.
The report cites requests on March 9 and August 13, 2026, that did not open the doors.
Even the Navy's own inspector general was blocked from the office's documents.
In September, the Pentagon inspector general sent a memo saying it would assess how the office's military staff did their jobs, The Washington Sun reported.
And then the sailors started talking.
Several sailors in the office had just been reassigned, Navy Times reported on November 12, according to The Washington Sun.
Navy Times reported that sailors were pressured to work 70 to 80 hour weeks, CBS said.
They described tasks such as "cutting a congressman's toenails or bringing their medication to their private residence at 3 a.m."
At least 4 people who worked in the office told Navy Times that mistreatment of sailors had been common over the past decade, The Washington Sun reported.
They said they feared losing monthly bonuses if they spoke up.
None of those people were named, and Monahan has not responded to those accounts.
So what exactly are investigators looking at now?
According to The Daily Beast, citing The Wall Street Journal, investigators are examining whether medications were dispensed to lawmakers and their staff without prescriptions.
Officials told the Journal his conduct has been under investigation for more than a year.
Which medications, and for whom, has not been made public.
Monahan has not been charged with any crime, and nothing in the report accuses him of one.
The Navy is now running its own inquiry, separate from the Pentagon's, CBS reported.
So what does a 17-year career end with?
First, the job itself: the only office in America whose patients are 535 members of Congress and nine Supreme Court justices.
Second, the access. For 17 years, his office saw the most powerful people in Washington, many of them in their 70s and 80s.
Third, his name on the record. The inspector general's findings are now public, with 5 recommendations still open.
And fourth, the Navy inquiry is still running, so his last chapter in uniform is not finished.
What has not been disclosed: whether he keeps his rank in retirement, and whether Congress will release its own records.
So has this happened before?
Then came the inspector general. And this is not the first time a Navy rear admiral who doctored the most powerful people in Washington was hit by one.

In March 2021, the Pentagon released an inspector general report on another Navy rear admiral: Ronny Jackson, the former White House physician, CBS News reported.
Investigators had opened the case in May 2018 and interviewed 78 witnesses.
The report described two incidents of inappropriate alcohol use on presidential trips, and his use of Ambien on long official flights.
Only 4 of 60 former subordinates said they never saw or heard him yell, curse or belittle staff, the report said.
Jackson rejected it. "I flat out reject any allegation that I consumed alcohol while on duty," he wrote. He called the report politically motivated.
His fall had started earlier. In 2018, President Trump nominated him to run Veterans Affairs. His name was pulled after reports about his conduct, CBS reported.
He retired from the Navy in 2019.
And in 2020 he won an open House seat in Texas's 13th District, after Rep. Mac Thornberry retired.
So the White House doctor who lost a cabinet job became a member of the same Congress that Monahan treated.
That is one way a fall can end. So where does Monahan's case stand?
Monahan's case and Jackson's case share one fact: both reports landed on a Navy two-star whose patients were the most powerful people in the country.
They differ in one fact too. Jackson's report was about his own behavior. Monahan's report is about how an office tracked its pills, and he is not accused of drinking or of any crime.
The office he leaves behind still has 5 physicians, 2 pharmacists and 11 corpsmen, and no named boss.
The next document is the Navy's own inquiry, with no release date announced.
And an older story shows how far these falls can reach. How far?
Monahan, Congress's doctor since 2009, is out, after a Pentagon report found controlled drugs were checked only 7 times in three years and the office blocked investigators.
On February 18, 2007, The Washington Post began a series by Dana Priest and Anne Hull on Building 18 at Walter Reed Army Medical Center.
Wounded soldiers described mold, rats and cockroaches, and some had no heat or water.
Army Secretary Francis Harvey relieved the hospital's commander, Major General George Weightman, a physician who had led Walter Reed for only six months.
Defense Secretary Robert Gates promised those responsible would be "held accountable."
On March 2, 2007, Gates forced Harvey himself to resign.
Then the fall reached the top doctor. The Army Surgeon General, Lieutenant General Kevin Kiley, had commanded Walter Reed before.
The Post reported he had known about the problems since 2003.
On March 12, 2007, Kiley retired after the acting Army Secretary asked for his resignation.
Three men were gone within about a month of one newspaper series.
The lesson of 2007: when military medicine fails the people it serves, the fall does not stop at the first name.
Will it stop at Monahan? That depends on what Congress does next.
The inspector general's 5 recommendations now sit with Congress and the Pentagon.
The first is an outside body to oversee the office's doctors, something the report says has been missing since 2013.
The second is written rules for the 11 corpsmen and the rest of the staff.
The third is a review of the pharmacy's inspection records, the ones that showed only 7 inventory checks in more than three years.
Congress also has to decide who treats 535 members while the Navy's inquiry runs.
This is the office that treats lawmakers at the Capitol every working day, and at the time of this report it had no announced leader.
And the House general counsel, which blocked investigators this year, has not said whether it will now release the records. Will it?
