"Cowboy" Bill Watts, the WWE Hall of Famer who ran Mid-South Wrestling and made the Junkyard Dog a superstar, has died at 87.
The news broke on November 17, 2026, through veteran wrestling journalist Bill Apter, who shared a message from Watts' son, Erik.
WWE confirmed it the same day. Watts played football for the Oklahoma Sooners, fought Bruno Sammartino, and later ran his own wrestling company out of Shreveport and Tulsa.
His Saturday show became the highest-rated program on Ted Turner's TBS, according to Wikipedia's history of the promotion.
In the next minutes: the night he put the world title on the first Black champion in wrestling history, the interview that ended his time at the top, and what his family has said.
The first word came from Bill Apter, the longtime wrestling magazine writer.
Sports Illustrated reports that Apter received a personal message from Erik Watts saying his father had passed away, and that Erik told Apter he loved him and held him in a very special place.
Ringside News says Apter was emotional reading it. Then WWE posted its statement: "WWE is saddened to learn that WWE Hall of Famer 'Cowboy' Bill Watts has passed away."
WWE extended its condolences to his family, friends and fans. Yahoo Sports reports that Triple H, who runs WWE's creative side, confirmed the news on November 17 and praised Watts' work as a performer, a promoter and an executive.
The family has not given a cause of death or a place.
No funeral date has been announced. But the WWE statement chose one phrase to sum him up, and anyone who saw him wrestle knows why.
WWE's statement called Watts "as tough as they came" inside the ring.
He earned that long before wrestling. He was born in Oklahoma City in 1939.
Bud Wilkinson recruited him to play guard for the Oklahoma Sooners. A near-fatal car accident with his mother left him in a coma during his junior year, Wikipedia notes.
He made it back. He then had a short stay with the Houston Oilers of the old American Football League, and a tryout with the Minnesota Vikings.
The Vikings' general manager, Jim Finks, wanted him to give up wrestling.
Watts decided he could make more money wrestling back home in Oklahoma.
He walked away from the NFL. And within a few years he was in the ring with the most famous champion in New York.
WWE's own tribute highlights his rivalry with Bruno Sammartino, the World Heavyweight Champion of the old WWWF.
Watts was big, loud and cocky, and New York crowds loved to hate him.
He never took the title from Sammartino. But he became one of the champion's best-known opponents.
He did win gold in New York, though. Watts held the WWWF United States Tag Team Championship with Gorilla Monsoon, one reign, according to Wikipedia.
He later held the North American Heavyweight title in Mid-South and the AWA United States title, Sports Illustrated reports.
He wrestled across the country. Then he did something few wrestlers ever managed.
He stopped working for promoters, and became one, and the company he built changed what you saw on Saturday television.
Watts bought Leroy McGuirk's NWA Tri-State territory in 1979 and renamed it Mid-South Wrestling.
He ran it from Shreveport, Louisiana, and Tulsa, Oklahoma. His idea was simple, and it changed the business.
Sports Illustrated credits him with pioneering episodic wrestling TV, storylines that continued week to week, like a soap opera with body slams.
WWE says he helped bring weekly televised wrestling stories in place of rare special events.
Viewers had to tune in next Saturday to see what happened. Jim Ross, later the voice of WWE, was his announcer.
When Ted Turner invited Watts onto TBS, Mid-South quickly became the network's highest-rated show, according to Wikipedia.
And the biggest reason fans tuned in was a man they called the Dog.
The Junkyard Dog was Mid-South's top draw. In New Orleans he was bigger than the football and basketball stars.
During one school year, the Junkyard Dog was more popular with New Orleans schoolchildren than Archie Manning and Pete Maravich, according to Wikipedia's history of the promotion.
One card with a "blinded" Junkyard Dog in the main event drew nearly 30,000 fans.
Another show drew 22,000. Watts built those nights with weeks of TV storylines.
He made fans believe the Dog could not see, and then made them pay to watch him fight anyway.
It was the Watts formula: tough men, simple stories and a reason to come back.
And the Dog was only the first star he built.
The list of names that came through Mid-South reads like a wrestling Hall of Fame.
News9 and WWE name "Hacksaw" Jim Duggan, Ted DiBiase, The Fabulous Freebirds, the Ultimate Warrior and Sting.
Sports Illustrated adds "Dr. Death" Steve Williams. Sting and the Warrior were tag partners there, before either became famous.
Dave Meltzer of the Wrestling Observer remembered Mid-South as one of the strongest TV wrestling programs of its era, Yahoo Sports reports.
Watts went national in March 1986 under a new name, the Universal Wrestling Federation, with two one-hour weekly shows in syndication.
It was a bold move against much bigger companies. And it nearly cost him everything he had built.

The national push cost Watts heavily. He sold the company after losing more than half a million dollars, according to Wikipedia.
On April 9, 1987, he sold the UWF to Jim Crockett Promotions.
Stars like Ted DiBiase and Jim Duggan had already gone to Vince McMahon's WWF.
In 1988, Ted Turner bought Crockett's company, and it became World Championship Wrestling.
So the territory Watts built ended up inside the company he would later run.
Watts stepped away from wrestling for years. When he came back, it was as the boss of WCW, and his first big decision made history.
On August 2, 1992, at the Baltimore Arena, Ron Simmons beat the 400-pound Vader with a powerslam to win the WCW World Heavyweight Championship.
Simmons became the first African American recognized as world heavyweight champion in wrestling, WWE says.
Watts was running WCW that night. Jim Ross, writing for WWE, said Watts believed the best performers should be on top regardless of race, and that he saw Simmons as an exceptional athlete.
In Ross's words, Watts "did not judge Ron by the color of his skin."
The packed crowd rose to its feet. Simmons has praised Watts for that decision in interviews.
It was one of the great nights of Watts' career. Within a year, his own words would end his run.
Watts became WCW's executive vice president in 1992, Sports Illustrated reports, and was out by the end of the next year.
The reason was an interview with the Pro Wrestling Torch newsletter. In it, according to Wikipedia and Yahoo Sports, Watts defended a restaurant owner's right to refuse service to Black customers and made other racist remarks.
Baseball legend Hank Aaron, who worked for Turner, objected, and Watts left.
Watts always said he resigned for his own reasons, and that his comments were taken out of context.
Yahoo Sports calls his legacy complicated, and it is: the same man who made Ron Simmons champion lost his job over those remarks.
He worked briefly for the WWF after that, as a booker. Then the family business passed to the next generation.
Watts trained his son Erik himself. Erik debuted in WCW in 1992 and won the Wrestling Observer's rookie of the year award that year, Sports Illustrated reports.
Erik's career ended in 2026. And it was Erik's message that brought the news of his father's death.
Watts also told his own story in a book. His autobiography, "The Cowboy and the Cross: The Bill Watts Story," came out from ECW Press.
The subtitle names three things: rebellion, wrestling and redemption. The title puts a cross right next to the cowboy.
And a few years after the book, WWE called his name.
On April 4, 2009, WWE inducted Bill Watts into its Hall of Fame.
Jim Ross, his old announcer from Mid-South, presented him, Sports Illustrated reports.
The Wrestling Observer had put him in its own Hall of Fame in 1996.
He never held the WWWF world title, but both halls count him among the greats.
WWE says he helped standardize turning journeymen into mainstays, building stars instead of buying them.
Many of the wrestlers you watched in the 80s and 90s learned the business in his territory.
And many of them wrote their goodbyes.
So here is where it stands. Bill Watts died at 87, his death announced on November 17.
He was a Sooner, a Sammartino rival, the boss of Mid-South and WCW, and a WWE Hall of Famer.
His legacy includes Junkyard Dog, Ron Simmons, and the interview that ended his run.
The family has not announced a funeral or memorial date. No cause of death has been released.
Watts was an Oklahoma man to the end; News9 reports he later lived in Bixby, just south of Tulsa.
Oklahoma City, where he was born, is where News9 first told his home state the news.
Tulsa and Shreveport were the two cities News9 says his company operated out of.
Tributes came in from the wrestlers he trained and the fans who watched him on Saturday mornings.
