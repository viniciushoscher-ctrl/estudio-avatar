The words on your Social Security statement were about to change. On November 20, 2026, President Trump signed the Claiming Age Clarity Act.
The phrase "full retirement age" is being retired. Age 62 becomes the "minimum monthly benefit age."
Age 67 becomes the "standard monthly benefit age." And age 70 becomes the "maximum monthly benefit age."
The Social Security Administration has one year to make the switch. In the next minutes: what changes and what does not, why Congress says the old words cost retirees money, and how much more you get by waiting.
The bill is H.R. 5284. According to the House Ways and Means Committee, it replaces the terms "early eligibility age," "full retirement age," "normal retirement age" and "delayed retirement credits" with three plain labels.
The minimum monthly benefit age is 62, the first age you can claim.
The standard monthly benefit age is the age most people call full retirement age.
And the maximum monthly benefit age is 70, when your monthly check stops growing.
NewsNation reports the agency has a year to update the terms in print and online, and BPC Action says that includes Social Security statements and educational materials.
But the most important part of the law is what it leaves alone.
Nothing about your money changes. NewsNation reports the new names do not affect when people can start collecting or how much they receive.
BPC Action says the law does not change taxes or benefit levels, and it does not speed up the trust fund's problems.
The age you can first claim is still 62. The standard age is still 67 for people born in 1960 or later, according to NewsNation and WVVA; the Ways and Means Committee says it is 66 or 67 depending on your birth year.
Medicare eligibility stays at 65, Newsweek notes. If you are already collecting, your check is exactly the same.
So why did Congress bother?
The people behind the bill say the old words mislead retirees. The problem is the word "full."
Many people hear "full retirement age" and think that is when they get the biggest check possible.
It is not. You can keep growing your check until 70. Ways and Means Chairman Jason Smith said, "Deciding when to claim Social Security is one of the biggest financial choices a senior will ever make."
He said the new law lets Americans see plainly what they gain by waiting and what they give up by claiming early.
Researchers at the University of Southern California tested this in a controlled experiment, BPC Action reports.
People shown the simpler labels understood the rules better and planned to claim slightly later.
And the numbers show how many people claim early.
According to Social Security data cited by NewsNation and IBTimes, about 53 percent of people awarded retirement benefits in 2025 received reduced checks because they claimed early.
BPC Action puts it another way: a majority of new beneficiaries claim before their full retirement age.
Some people need the money, or have health reasons, and claiming early is a legal and sometimes smart choice.
But the law's backers say others claim early simply because they misunderstand the words.
And the difference in dollars is large.
NewsNation, citing the Social Security Administration, gives a simple example. Say your check at 67 would be 1,000 dollars a month.
Claim at 62, and you get about 700 dollars, a 30 percent cut, for life.
Wait until 70, and you get about 1,240 dollars, 24 percent more.
Compare 62 to 70, and that is more than 75 percent more every month, BPC Action says, and the higher amount can carry over to a surviving spouse.
Social Security's own averages for retired workers in June 2026 show the gap in real checks: about 1,452 dollars a month at age 62, about 2,038 at 67, and about 2,265 at 70, according to NewsNation and IBTimes.
Those are averages across different people, not the same person, but the direction is clear.
So who wrote this law, and who voted against it?
Almost nobody voted against it. The House bill was introduced by Republican Lloyd Smucker of Pennsylvania and Democrat Don Beyer of Virginia.
In the Senate, Republicans Bill Cassidy and Susan Collins joined Democrats Tim Kaine and Chris Coons.
The Ways and Means Committee approved it 41 to 1. The full House passed it by voice vote, the committee says, and the Senate passed it by unanimous consent.
Smucker said it will make it easier for Americans who have worked hard their entire lives to make informed decisions.
Kaine said he looks forward to the positive impact on Americans' financial security.
AARP endorsed it. So did the Association of Mature American Citizens and BPC Action.
In Washington, a 41 to 1 vote is rare. But the people who backed it are already warning that renaming the ages is the easy part.
The new names arrive at the same time as a bigger check.
The 2027 cost-of-living adjustment is 3.6 percent, the biggest in four years, Fortune reports, and the average retired worker's check rises to about 2,170 dollars a month.
But Medicare's standard Part B premium also rises 6 dollars and 50 cents, to 209 dollars and 40 cents a month, starting in January 2027, according to Fortune, citing the Medicare agency's November 17 announcement.
On a 1,000-dollar check, Fortune calculates, the premium increase eats about 18 percent of the raise.
On the average check, it eats about 9 percent. That math is one more reason the timing of your claim matters: the bigger your monthly check, the smaller the share a flat premium takes out of each raise.
And the people who wrote the new law are betting that clearer words will push more retirees toward a bigger check.

The research behind the bill is a 2023 study led by researchers at the University of Southern California, published in the Journal of Pension Economics and Finance under the title "Three little words."
BPC Action says the researchers called relabeling a cost-effective fix. Smucker and Beyer introduced the House bill in September 2025.
BPC Action notes that in 2024, 62 percent of new beneficiaries claimed before full retirement age.
The Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget called the law a good first step, according to NewsNation.
In other words, it took about three years to go from a study to a signature.
And the next problem on Social Security's list is far bigger than a name.
IBTimes reports the Act contains no measures to address Social Security's projected funding gap.
NewsNation reports that retirees could see a 22 percent benefit cut in late 2032 if Congress does not act.
BPC Action's president, Michele Stockwell, said the law shows bipartisan progress on Social Security is possible, and that lawmakers must use this momentum to tackle trust fund solvency.
Fortune adds the background: the 2026 Trustees Report says the main retirement trust fund's reserves run out in the last quarter of 2032, leaving enough to pay 78 percent of scheduled benefits.
There were about 2.6 workers paying in for every beneficiary in 2025, Fortune reports, compared with 41.9 in 1945, and the number is projected to fall to about 1.9 by 2075.
So the same Congress that agreed on three new names faces a much harder vote on money.
And that brings the question back to your own kitchen table.
If you already collect Social Security, the law does not change your check, your start date or your Medicare.
What changes is the paperwork you will see over the year after the signing, and the language your children and grandchildren will read when they decide.
For a spouse who has not claimed yet, the new label "maximum monthly benefit age" makes the trade-off plain: waiting to 70 means the largest monthly check, and BPC Action notes the larger amount can protect a surviving spouse.
Whether waiting is right depends on health, savings and family, and nothing in the law forces anyone to wait.
But one group of Americans will notice the change first.
The people who will see the new words first are those turning 62 after the switch.
For them, NewsNation reports, the standard monthly benefit age is 67. They will be the first to read "minimum," "standard" and "maximum" on their statements instead of "early," "full" and "delayed."
The agency had not announced a rollout schedule at the time of this report, Newsweek reports, but the deadline is November 20, 2027.
That year will show how fast Social Security can rewrite its own vocabulary.
Here is what we know. The Claiming Age Clarity Act was signed on November 20, 2026.
Age 62 is the minimum monthly benefit age, 67 the standard, and 70 the maximum.
Social Security has one year to switch. Your ages, your checks and your Medicare do not change.
And the 2032 funding gap is still waiting.
Watch for three things. First, new wording on Social Security statements and on the agency's website over the twelve months after the signing.
Second, whether Congress takes up the funding gap, which BPC Action and others are pushing.
Third, the next official numbers on how many people claim at 62.
If the law works, that share should start to fall.
