Jensen Huang lost about 108 million dollars by quitting Nvidia, Bloomberg reported. That is the value of the stock awards he had not yet earned, and company policy says they are gone.
He has not been accused of any crime.
In the next minutes: what Huang still keeps, the three things nobody has disclosed yet, and how it ended for the CEOs who fell before him at HP, Intel, McDonald's and CBS.
Huang held about 346,000 restricted stock units that had not vested yet, Bloomberg reported.
He was also in line for about 310,000 more shares if Nvidia hit certain performance targets.
Nvidia's rule is simple. Unvested restricted stock units are forfeited once an executive's "continuous service" ends.
Huang's service ended on November 12, 2026, when Nvidia announced his resignation, effective immediately.
So those awards, worth about 108 million dollars by Bloomberg's count, are gone.
But for Huang, 108 million dollars is a small number. How small?
The Bloomberg Billionaires Index puts Huang's fortune at 138.4 billion dollars.
The 108 million dollars he forfeits is about 0.08% of that, Bloomberg reported.
His cash pay was also small next to his wealth: about 7.5 million dollars in each of the past two years.
Almost all of his money is in one place: Nvidia shares he already owns outright.
And those, nobody can take away. So what exactly does he keep?
Huang owns about 3.5% of Nvidia, Bloomberg reported. That stake makes up nearly 97% of his fortune.
These are ordinary shares, not options. They are his.
Nvidia has only one class of shares, so Huang has no special voting power, Bloomberg noted. His votes count like any other shareholder's.
He co-founded the company more than three decades ago, and he remains its largest individual shareholder, Newsweek reported.
But owning the shares means every drop in the stock hits him first. How hard did it hit?
The day he quit, Nvidia shares fell as much as 14%, then closed down 4.3% at 166 dollars and 19 cents.
That one day wiped out more than 6 billion dollars of Huang's net worth, Bloomberg reported.
The next day, Nvidia fell again, closing at 162 dollars and 85 cents, The Motley Fool reported.
So the stock losses in two days dwarf the 108 million dollars in awards he gave up.
So what has Nvidia still not disclosed about his exit?
First: severance. Neither Bloomberg nor any filing we have seen says whether Huang receives a severance payment. Nvidia has not said.
Second: the board. It has not been reported whether Huang keeps his seat on Nvidia's board of directors.
Third: clawbacks. It has not been reported whether Nvidia will try to take back any pay he already received.
That third question matters, because in one famous case, a company did exactly that, and recovered more than 100 million dollars.
Before that case, what about the lawsuit already filed against Huang?
Shareholders have sued Nvidia and Huang in federal court in San Jose, CBS News and Reuters reported.
They say the company and Huang misled investors about his ties to a Singapore businesswoman whom U.S. officials reportedly suspect of working for Chinese intelligence. China calls that "groundless." Her former company denies wrongdoing.
The suit covers investors who bought between October 9 and November 8. Nvidia lost about 20% in that window, roughly 1 trillion dollars of market value, Reuters reported.
None of it has been proven in court.
So who is now running Nvidia, and what does she face first?
Nvidia named its chief financial officer, Colette Kress, interim CEO, the company said.
Her first test was set for November 17, 2026, when Nvidia was due to report quarterly earnings, its first without Huang as CEO.
Kress keeps her finance job at the same time, Newsweek reported. And for the first time ever, Nvidia's board has a chair: Harvey Jones.
So who decides what Huang gets paid on the way out? Which law firm did the board hire?
Nvidia's independent directors hired the Wall Street law firm Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen and Katz on November 10, 2026, Reuters reported.
An independent committee of directors is reviewing everything. Nvidia says the board has "no reason to believe" national security or company secrets were affected.
If that committee finds the board missed warning signs, shareholders could sue the directors themselves, and directors could be ordered to repay fines or settlements, Reuters explained.
That same committee is the group that would decide any severance, or any attempt to claw back pay.
So what is Wall Street betting will happen next?
Several big firms slashed their targets for Nvidia stock after he quit, The Motley Fool reported.
Bernstein cut from 400 dollars to 200. Blair went from 500 to 200. Melius Research went from 315 to 185.
Evercore cut from 465 to 186, saying Nvidia "has no obvious succession plan or number two operational executive."
But Raymond James kept 515 dollars, saying Nvidia "is more than just its CEO."
The middle of all those targets is 277 dollars and 50 cents, against the close of 162 dollars and 85 cents the day after he quit.
At about 12 times next year's expected earnings, the stock is the cheapest it has been in 15 years, The Motley Fool reported.
So the market is betting on Nvidia without Huang. Has the last CEO who quit like this taught investors anything?

Intel's CEO, Brian Krzanich, resigned on June 21, 2018, ABC7 reported.
Intel said he had a past, consensual relationship with an employee, which broke the company's "non-fraternization policy" for all managers.
He had joined Intel as an engineer in 1982 and became CEO in 2013.
Intel named its chief financial officer, Robert Swan, interim CEO, effective immediately. Intel shares fell about 2% that morning.
That is almost exactly what Nvidia did, with Colette Kress. So where does Huang, 63, go from here?
Huang is 63, Bloomberg reported, and he has not announced his next role.
Elon Musk has publicly said he would hire him, Billionaires.Africa reported.
Huang has agreed to testify before House committees, and Senate Banking Chair Tim Scott wants him by December 3, Newsweek reported.
So how fast can a fallen CEO land a new job? At HP, it took weeks.
HP's CEO, Mark Hurd, resigned on August 6, 2010, the AP reported.
HP's board found he had falsified expense reports to hide a relationship with a woman who did marketing work for the company. HP said its harassment policy was not violated. The woman's lawyer said there was "no affair and no intimate sexual relationship."
Hurd still walked away with a 12.2 million dollar severance payment and nearly 350,000 HP shares, worth about 16 million dollars, the AP reported.
HP stock fell 9.7% after hours.
Six weeks later, he had a new job. Who hired him?
Oracle hired Hurd as co-president, reporting to Larry Ellison, the AP reported on September 20, 2010.
His Oracle deal included a 950,000 dollar salary, a possible 10 million dollar cash bonus, and 10 million stock options.
To settle HP's lawsuit over the move, Hurd gave up restricted HP stock worth nearly 14 million dollars.
So one fallen CEO kept millions and landed on his feet.
The next one was not so lucky. He had to give back more than 100 million.
Huang forfeits about 108 million dollars in unvested awards, keeps about 3.5% of Nvidia, and his severance, board seat and any clawback are still not disclosed.
McDonald's fired its CEO, Steve Easterbrook, in November 2019, after he admitted a consensual relationship with an employee, Fox Business reported.
At first, the board signed a "without cause" deal that let him keep stock that he would otherwise have lost.
Then an internal investigation found other undisclosed relationships with employees. McDonald's sued him in August 2020.
In 2021, Easterbrook paid back more than 105 million dollars in stock and cash to McDonald's.
"We fired him, and then sued him upon learning that he lied about his behavior," McDonald's said.
So did the government come after him too?
In January 2023, Easterbrook agreed to pay the Securities and Exchange Commission a 400,000 dollar penalty, without admitting or denying its claims, Fox Business reported.
The SEC said he misled investors about why he left.
He was barred for five years from serving as an officer or director of any public company.
The SEC can investigate Nvidia's own disclosures too, Reuters noted. It declined to comment.
But the biggest payout ever denied to a fallen CEO was not at McDonald's. It was at a TV network.
The CBS board refused to pay its former CEO, Les Moonves, his 120 million dollar severance on December 17, 2018, ABC7 reported.
The board said it had grounds to fire him for cause, citing "willful and material misfeasance" and his "willful failure to cooperate fully" with its investigation.
CBS had already given 20 million dollars of that money to 18 groups fighting workplace harassment.
Moonves' lawyer said he "vehemently denies any non-consensual sexual relations."
So which of these paths is Huang on? The dates that followed would tell.
Nvidia's next earnings report was set for November 17, 2026. Any severance or board decision could appear in the company's filings.
On November 18, interim CEO Colette Kress was set to hold a company-wide meeting, according to her memo reported by Tom's Guide.
Senate Banking Chair Tim Scott asked Huang to testify by December 3, 2026, Newsweek reported.
Will Huang end up like Hurd, who kept millions, or like Easterbrook, who paid them back?
