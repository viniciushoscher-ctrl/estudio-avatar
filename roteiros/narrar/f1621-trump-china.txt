President Trump left the White House on November 16, 2026, for a trip that ends with something no president has ever done: a single meeting with the leaders of China and Russia at the same table.
On November 19 in Shenzhen, Trump, Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin are set to talk about cutting nuclear weapons and about the war in Iran.
And as he left, Trump dropped a surprise. Asked about North Korea's Kim Jong Un, he said, "He may come.
I don't know. That's up to President Xi." In the next minutes: what is really on the table in Shenzhen, why a Kim meeting is harder than it sounds, and how the last big trips by Nixon, Reagan and Trump himself turned out.
Trump's line about Kim landed before Air Force One was even in the air.
ABC News reports there are real obstacles. Kim rarely flies. To meet Trump in Singapore in 2018, he borrowed a Boeing 747 from China.
Since then, his long trips abroad have been by armored train. North Korea's trains average about 35 miles an hour, and reach about 50 on China's faster tracks.
A one-way train trip from Pyongyang to Shenzhen could take up to 60 hours.
That means Kim would have to leave by the night of November 16 to make it by November 19.
In October, Chinese state media said Kim made a short, quiet car trip into China over a new bridge, and he did not meet Xi.
North Korea is not a member of APEC, and Kim has never attended an APEC meeting.
South Korea's top diplomat reportedly said there is little chance he will come.
So the Kim question stays open, but the schedule for November 19 was already set.
Here is the route. Trump left Washington on November 16. On November 17 he lands in the Philippines for the ASEAN summit of Southeast Asian nations.
Late on November 18 he arrives in Shenzhen, the city across the border from Hong Kong, for the APEC summit.
On November 19 comes the main event. First, a working lunch and a meeting with Xi Jinping.
It will be their fourth meeting since Trump returned to office in 2025.
Xi came to Washington in September. Then comes the three-way meeting with Xi and Putin.
There is no separate one-on-one with Putin on the schedule. Elon Musk traveled with the president, but the White House said he would not sit in on the meeting with Xi and Putin.
The Kremlin confirmed the meeting on November 14 and called it "extremely significant."
China's state media mentioned Xi's bilateral talks, but said nothing about the three-way meeting.
The first item Trump wants on the table is the one that matters most to anyone who grew up with duck-and-cover drills.
White House spokeswoman Olivia Wales put it plainly. "The president has made it clear that, among other topics, he would discuss the reduction of nuclear weapons in the world."
Trump believes their spread is "very dangerous," she said. The timing matters.
The last nuclear arms treaty between the United States and Russia expired in February, the BBC reports.
For the first time in decades, the two largest arsenals have no major limits.
The latest public estimates from the Federation of American Scientists put the U.S. at about 3,700 warheads, Russia at about 4,400 and China at about 620.
Together that is more than 8,000. The Pentagon has projected China could reach 1,000 warheads by 2030.
Trump has said he could replace the expired treaty with a better deal that includes China.
The United Nations has urged a new framework quickly. But a senior U.S. official told the BBC no agreement was expected from the talks, and that sets up the second topic.
The second topic is Iran. "I think the president does intend to talk about Iran in the trilateral meeting with Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin," a senior U.S. official said on a call with reporters.
China is the biggest buyer of Iran's oil, and Russia is a close military partner of Tehran, so their leaders have weight Washington does not.
Ukraine is a different story. U.S. officials say it is not expected to come up.
Washington has what one official called a "whole other track" of talks with Kyiv and Moscow, and China is not part of it.
U.S. envoys have recently met with both Putin and Ukraine's President Zelensky.
The Iran war has already shaped politics at home. After the midterms, former Vice President Mike Pence said Vice President JD Vance was "wrong" to blame the war for Republican losses, according to The Hill.
So the war was on the table in Shenzhen. And some topics are being kept off it on purpose.
Chips are off the table. A second U.S. official said, "We do not negotiate American export controls."
That matters because of the Nvidia scandal. The company's former CEO, Jensen Huang, stepped down in November 2026 after reports about his relationship with a woman U.S. intelligence suspects of passing information to Beijing.
Huang has not been charged, and China calls the claims groundless. The same official told AFP, "The situation with Nvidia should not really have any bearing on the trip."
Ukraine is off the table. A separate Trump-Putin one-on-one is not on the schedule.
What is left is a short list: nuclear weapons, Iran, and trade with Xi.
The Kremlin's spokesman, Dmitry Peskov, already pushed back on one comparison people keep making, to a meeting of three leaders that happened before most of our viewers were born.
Presidents have made this kind of trip before. On February 21, 1972, Richard Nixon landed in China.
He stayed eight days and visited Beijing, Hangzhou and Shanghai. Soon after he arrived, Chairman Mao Zedong met him.
On February 27, the two sides issued the Shanghai Communiqué, agreeing to work toward normal relations while admitting they still disagreed over Taiwan.
Nixon called it "the week that changed the world." Full diplomatic relations took seven more years, until 1979 under President Carter.
Some commentators compared Shenzhen to Yalta in 1945, where Roosevelt, Churchill and Stalin planned the postwar world, CBS News notes.
Peskov rejected that, saying agreements made in Shenzhen cannot be forced on others.
The lesson from 1972 is that the first meeting rarely settles anything.
What it can do is start something, and the White House is promising exactly that.
Olivia Wales said the meeting would "secure historic wins" for Americans and advance U.S. interests in the Indo-Pacific.
Supporters of the president see a chance no other leader has: to put the world's three biggest military powers in one room on nuclear weapons.
Critics and many former officials are more cautious. Former senior U.S. officials and analysts told the BBC that bringing Beijing and Moscow into arms talks could face major obstacles and take years.
China has long refused to join U.S.-Russia arms limits, saying its arsenal is far smaller.
Russia wants Ukraine kept separate, and so does Washington. And no deal was expected.
So the real test is not the photo. It is whether the three agree to keep talking, with a date and a format.
The last time a president and Moscow did that, it led to the only treaty in history that scrapped an entire class of nuclear missiles.

Here is where it stands. Trump left on November 16. He stopped in the Philippines on November 17, landed in Shenzhen on November 18, and was set to meet Xi and then Xi and Putin together on November 19.
Nuclear cuts and Iran are on the table. Ukraine and chips are not.
And Kim Jong Un "may come," but the trip would be long and hard.
In October 1986, Ronald Reagan and Mikhail Gorbachev met in Reykjavík, Iceland, and agreed in principle to pull intermediate-range nuclear missiles out of Europe.
Fourteen months later, on December 8, 1987, at 1:45 in the afternoon, they signed the INF Treaty at the White House.
The Senate approved it 93 to 5 in May 1988. By the deadline in June 1991, 2,692 missiles had been destroyed, 846 American and 1,846 Soviet.
It was the first time the two sides got rid of a whole category of weapons.
The treaty did not last forever. The United States withdrew on August 2, 2019, during Trump's first term, citing Russian violations.
Trump is the one asking Moscow, and this time Beijing too, to cut again.
The difference is the third chair at the table, and the man who may or may not be on a train heading south.
Ukraine's President Zelensky was not going to be in Shenzhen. Neither were Europe's leaders.
North Korea is not an APEC member, so Kim would come only as Xi's guest, if at all.
U.S. officials say Washington's Ukraine talks run on their own track. That leaves three men deciding what to discuss about weapons that can reach every country on earth.
Supporters say that is exactly the point: only these three can make real cuts.
Critics say talks without allies can leave Europe and Asia nervous. Both sides agree on one thing: that meeting is a beginning, not an end.
And there is one man who has already been through a beginning like this with Trump, and knows how the sequel went.
On June 12, 2018, Trump and Kim Jong Un met at the Capella Hotel on Sentosa Island in Singapore.
It was the first meeting ever between a sitting U.S. president and North Korea's leader.
Kim flew there on an Air China 747. They signed a four-point statement: new relations, a lasting peace on the Korean Peninsula, work toward "complete denuclearization," and the return of the remains of American prisoners of war and missing soldiers.
A second summit followed in Hanoi in February 2019. North Korea still has its nuclear weapons.
For veterans of the Korean War and their families, the remains promise was the part that mattered most.
Singapore showed what a first meeting can deliver: a handshake, a promise and a photo.
What it delivers next depends on what happens after the cameras leave, and that brings us to the calendar.
November 17: Trump at the ASEAN summit in the Philippines. Night of November 18: arrival in Shenzhen.
November 19: the working lunch and meeting with Xi, then the first-ever meeting of the American, Chinese and Russian leaders.
Watch three things. First, whether the three agree on a follow-up date for nuclear talks.
Second, whether Iran gets any joint statement. Third, whether Kim Jong Un leaves Pyongyang, which would show up on the rails days before he arrives.
U.S. officials have already lowered expectations, saying no agreement is expected. The Kremlin calls the meeting "extremely significant."
Beijing has said the least. By November 20 we would know whether Shenzhen is remembered like Nixon in Beijing, or like Trump and Kim in Hanoi.
