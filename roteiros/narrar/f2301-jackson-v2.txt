Tom Jackson has died at 75. For fourteen seasons he was a linebacker for the Denver Broncos, a cornerstone of the defense fans called the Orange Crush.
For nearly three decades after that, he sat next to Chris Berman on ESPN and walked millions of men through their football Sundays.
His family and ESPN announced the death on November 22, 2026, at the start of the very show he helped build.
In the next minutes: how the news was broken on live television, what Berman said when he could barely say it, and the Broncos record that stood when he walked away.
According to CBS News and the Associated Press, Tom Jackson died on November 20, 2026.
His family and ESPN announced it on November 22. CBS reported that the family asked that the death not be made public until the start of Sunday NFL Countdown, the pregame show where he spent so many autumn mornings.
ESPN reported that Jackson's family informed the network of his death. Men's Journal reported that Chris Berman delivered the news himself on the November 22 edition of the show.
So the people who heard it first were exactly the people who had watched him for years: fans sitting down with coffee before the early games.
The Denver Broncos followed with their own statement within hours. That timing was a choice by the family, and it told you where they believed he belonged.
But the hardest part of the morning was the man who had to read it.
Chris Berman worked beside Jackson for most of his television life. According to ESPN and Newsweek, Berman called the death unexpected and said, "This is a shock."
He said he was fortunate to work side by side with Tom for thirty years, primarily on NFL Primetime and Sunday NFL Countdown.
Then he said the line every outlet quoted: "We've lost one of the best human beings ever to walk the planet, Tom Jackson."
And he added, "Tom was my best friend professionally and personally." What has not been said matters too.
As of November 22, the cause of death had not been made public; Men's Journal reported that it had not been revealed.
We will not guess. The family has not given one, and ESPN has not given one.
What ESPN did give was a statement from the very top of the company.
ESPN chairman Jimmy Pitaro called Jackson "one of the most influential and beloved figures in ESPN's history."
He said, "Alongside Chris Berman, he helped make NFL Primetime an iconic part of our football weekends."
There is a physical reminder of that partnership. ESPN's NFL studio in Bristol, Connecticut, is named the Chris Berman-Tom Jackson Studio, according to CBS.
Very few broadcasters get a room named after them while they are alive.
Jackson joined ESPN in 1987, the year after his last season, and he did not leave until his final assignment, the Pro Football Hall of Fame enshrinement ceremony.
Even then he came back once more, for a digital-only edition of NFL Primetime with Berman.
ESPN's Mike Greenberg put him in the company of Howard Cosell, the man who once did the halftime highlights on Monday Night Football.
But Denver fans had a number of their own to remember him by.
That number is 191. According to ESPN's Jeff Legwold, Jackson played 191 regular-season games for the Broncos, with 177 starts, and that was the most games in franchise history when he retired.
He added ten playoff games. NFL.com and Newsweek list his career totals at 1,531 tackles, 44 sacks, 20 interceptions, three of them returned for touchdowns, and three fumble recoveries.
Those 20 interceptions tied Randy Gradishar for the most by a Broncos linebacker, ESPN reported.
He went to three Pro Bowls and earned Associated Press All-Pro honors.
He spent every one of his fourteen seasons in one city, wearing one uniform.
In the modern league, a linebacker staying with one team that long almost never happens.
And when the news broke, the reaction came from far beyond Denver.
Commissioner Roger Goodell said, according to Newsweek, "The NFL lost a wonderful man with the passing of Tom Jackson."
Former ESPN colleague Louis Riddick remembered "the firm handshake, the smile, the laugh, the eye contact."
ESPN's Field Yates wrote that "TJ alongside Boomer would present the full day of football to us for the first time."
That is the part younger fans forget. Before phones and highlight apps, Sunday night was the only place you could see every game you missed, and Jackson was the man explaining it.
Randy Moss told viewers, according to CBS, that he remembered rushing home from games to catch the show.
Newsweek also listed tributes from Louisville football, Deion Sanders, Rich Eisen and CBS Sports.
The Broncos, though, wrote the statement that said the most about who he was.
The Broncos said they were "heartbroken by the passing of Ring of Fame linebacker Tom Jackson, a cornerstone of the legendary Orange Crush defense."
They added, "Above all, TJ loved the Broncos and was a cherished member of our family for more than 50 years."
TJ was what teammates and viewers called him. Jackson himself once said he was blessed to work for only two companies in his life: the Denver Broncos and ESPN.
He also once said, at a Broncos training camp, "I wouldn't have traded my time in football with the Broncos for anything."
The team's late defensive coordinator, Joe Collier, once called him "one of the most important players in that group and our emotional leader."
So how did a fourth-round pick become the emotional leader of the most famous defense of its era?
Before we go back to the beginning, tell us in the comments: did you watch Tom Jackson and Chris Berman on NFL Primetime on Sunday nights, or do you remember him in the orange jersey first?
We read every answer.
Jackson came out of the University of Louisville. The Broncos took him in the fourth round of the 1973 draft, according to CBS and ESPN.
Fourth-round linebackers are not supposed to start for long, and many never start at all.
He did more than start. In 1976 he intercepted seven passes, still the Broncos' single-season record for a linebacker, ESPN reported.
He was among the team's top five tacklers for eleven straight seasons, and in 1980 he made 180 tackles.
That was the kind of player Collier was talking about: not the biggest name, but the one who never came off the field.
And every one of those seasons was building toward a single winter.
Most players who move to television never earn the honor Jackson earned there.
In 2015 the Pro Football Hall of Fame gave him the Pete Rozelle Radio-Television Award, which recognizes long-time contributions to broadcasting the game.
According to ESPN, it came five years after Berman received the same honor, which put the two partners side by side in Canton's broadcasters' record just as they sat side by side on November 22.
Newsweek also reports that Jackson won a Sports Emmy for Outstanding Studio Analyst.
The style was simple: he talked to the viewer the way a linebacker talks in a huddle, short and direct, and he never pretended a bad team was good.
But viewers who are old enough remember where his reputation started.
Where were you in January 1978? According to Newsweek, Jackson helped lead the Broncos to the franchise's first Super Bowl after the 1977 season.
Denver had never been there before. The opponent was the Dallas Cowboys, in Super Bowl XII, and Dallas won.
ESPN reported that Jackson was a first-team All-Pro that season. For Denver, the season itself was the victory: a city that had never had a contender suddenly had a defense the whole country was talking about, and the orange jerseys filled the stands.
Newsweek notes he made it back to the big game nine seasons later, after the 1986 season, when Denver lost to the New York Giants in Super Bowl XXI.
Two trips, no ring. And then he made the decision every player faces.
When he retired after his fourteenth season, Jackson was in his mid-thirties and the oldest linebacker in the league, according to ESPN.
Asked why he stopped, he said, "it was just time." There was no farewell tour and no long goodbye.
He simply moved from the field to the studio, and within months he was at ESPN.
The Broncos put him in their Ring of Fame years later, the team's highest honor.
His teammates on that defense became household names in Denver, and Jackson stayed the link between them and the country, because every Sunday night he was the one on television.
That is why his death landed differently from a typical player's. Millions of men did not just watch him play.
They spent their weekends with him.
The show was NFL Primetime, and later Sunday NFL Countdown in the mornings.
Berman supplied the nicknames and the noise; Jackson supplied the verdict. ESPN's tenure figures differ by source, from 29 years to 32, but every outlet agrees he joined in 1987 and stayed until 2016, when he worked the Hall of Fame enshrinement as his last assignment.
In 2019 he returned for the digital-only NFL Primetime with Berman, according to CBS and ESPN.
For a generation of fans, that pairing was the sound of a football weekend ending.
One more Canton moment showed how much the game still trusted him.
According to NFL.com, Jackson starred alongside Pro Football Hall of Fame linebacker Randy Gradishar, and it was Jackson who presented Gradishar for enshrinement in Canton in 2024.
CBS published a photo of the two men together at that induction ceremony.
Gradishar had waited decades for that call, and when it finally came, he chose his old Orange Crush partner to stand beside him.
In a way, Jackson's own career was told through other people's honors: Berman's award, Gradishar's bust, the Broncos' Ring of Fame.
The honors were being written for him.
Here is what we know. Tom Jackson died on November 20 at 75.
His family and ESPN announced it at the start of Sunday NFL Countdown.
The cause has not been disclosed. He played 191 games for Denver, the team record when he retired, and spent about three decades at ESPN beside Chris Berman.
No funeral or memorial plans had been announced in the reports we found.
According to CBS and ESPN, he is survived by his wife and two daughters.
With the season in full swing, expect tributes at Broncos home games and on ESPN's NFL shows in the coming weeks.
We will report the memorial plans when the family announces them. Randy Gradishar, the Hall of Famer Jackson presented in Canton, remains one of the faces of that Orange Crush defense still with us.
And in Bristol, the studio still carries his name.
Where it stands: an Orange Crush linebacker and a Sunday-morning voice, gone at 75.
