An Army major who survived the deadliest Iranian attack on American troops in the Iran war says his leaders were warned, and did not act.
Maj. Stephen Ramsbottom has filed a whistleblower complaint with the House and Senate Armed Services Committees.
He says the post in Kuwait where six soldiers died on March 1 had nothing to stop a drone, and his complaint says it had "no physician, no fixed aid station, and only one ambulance."
President Trump answered on November 16, saying 99.99 percent of Iranian missiles and drones have been stopped or caused no U.S. deaths.
In the next minutes: what the major says happened that morning, what the Pentagon and the President say, and how the military answered the same question after Beirut and Khobar Towers.
Port Shuaiba is an industrial port south of Kuwait City, about 10 miles from Camp Arifjan.
On March 1, about a day after the United States and Israel launched Operation Epic Fury, soldiers there were working out of trailers.
Around 9 in the morning, an Iranian one-way attack drone flew in low and slow and hit the top of a triple-wide trailer.
No warning siren sounded. Ramsbottom told IBTimes the strike came about 30 minutes after troops got an all-clear.
Six Army Reserve soldiers were killed. More than 30 were wounded, with brain injuries, shrapnel wounds and burns.
The night before, Ramsbottom says, soldiers saw five quadcopters circling the site and suspected they were being watched.
What he says he told his superiors before that morning is the heart of the complaint.
The trailers sat behind concrete T-walls. Those walls are built to stop car bombs and ground attacks.
"There was nothing there designed to protect from an aerial attack," Ramsbottom said.
He says he raised the lack of protection from the air with his superiors, and was told not to worry.
A command sergeant major told the troops a counter-drone system would be sent, he says.
It never arrived. Sources told CNN the building that was hit had no protection against drones or missiles.
And then there is the medical side. The complaint says the post had no doctor, no fixed aid station and one ambulance.
Ramsbottom does not blame only others. "I feel this is a tactical failure by the Army, our leadership, and myself, all the way down to my level," he told 60 Minutes.
"We just did not plan this well, and this needs to be a lesson that we learn."
The six names behind that lesson are public.
They came from the 103rd Expeditionary Sustainment Command, based in Des Moines, Iowa.
Captain Cody Khork, 35, of Winter Haven, Florida. Sergeant First Class Noah Tietjens, 42, of Bellevue, Nebraska.
Sergeant First Class Nicole Amor, 39, of White Bear Lake, Minnesota. Sergeant Declan Coady, 20, of West Des Moines, Iowa, promoted after his death.
Major Jeffrey O'Brien, 45, of Indianola, Iowa. And Chief Warrant Officer 3 Robert Marzan, 54, of Sacramento, California.
They were the first American soldiers killed in the war. On March 7, President Trump and Vice President Vance were at Dover Air Force Base for the dignified transfer.
Eight months later, the families are hearing from a survivor that the danger was raised before the strike.
And the wounded say their fight did not end that morning.
Medics removed shrapnel from Ramsbottom's head. He told the Associated Press he then waited more than four months for his MRI results.
"I've had to build my own case for everything," he said. "It's very frustrating."
In October, several wounded soldiers, including Ramsbottom, said the military health system had not properly supported their recovery, Military.com reports.
When the attack happened, the Pentagon first reported five seriously wounded and others with minor injuries.
CBS later reported more than 30 seriously injured, some treated in Germany at Landstuhl and some in the United States.
For a generation that remembers how wounded veterans were treated after Vietnam, that gap will sound familiar.
And on November 16, the President of the United States answered the report himself.
President Trump posted on Truth Social on November 16, 2026. "A recent study on an attack in the early stages of Iran was not correctly stated," he wrote.
"Translation: 99.99 percent of Iranian missiles and drones have been shot down or, in any event, not caused U.S. deaths."
He wrote that "CENTCOM defensive measures have been historically effective." According to his post, Iran has fired 8,000 to 9,000 one-way attack drones, and only two got through to cause American deaths, one in Kuwait and one in Saudi Arabia.
He wrote that roughly 2,000 ballistic missiles were fired, and one got past defenses, killing Americans in Jordan.
He also called 60 Minutes "even worse than their predecessors." Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth shared a screenshot of the President's post on X, The Epoch Times reported.
Supporters say the numbers show the defenses worked. The major's point is different: one of those two drones found a post that, he says, had nothing to stop it.
And what Hegseth said the day after the strike was read again.
At a press conference the day after the attack, Hegseth said the troops were well protected.
He called the drone a "squirter" that "makes its way through," CBS reports, and said it hit a fortified tactical operations center.
He called a drone getting through a rare event, and said, "these are powerful weapons."
Pentagon spokesman Sean Parnell disputed reports at the time that the site was makeshift, and said every possible measure had been taken.
A Pentagon spokesperson declined to comment to CBS on whether force protection at Port Shuaiba was adequate.
CENTCOM has confirmed an investigation. A preliminary Army memo said Iranian intelligence and allied groups likely tracked U.S. personnel moving to smaller sites.
None of the major's claims has been independently confirmed. But they reached a senator, and he wanted the files.
Senator Richard Blumenthal, Democrat of Connecticut, wrote to Hegseth in a letter dated November 15.
He asked the Secretary to come to a classified briefing and hand over "all force protection assessments" for Port Shuaiba.
He called for transparency and accountability for the families, the wounded and the public.
Democrats have said they will investigate how the administration handled the war once they control both houses of Congress in 2027.
Republicans and the White House point to the numbers in the President's post and to CENTCOM's air defenses.
Both sides had a sworn complaint from a wounded officer in front of the Armed Services Committees.
The military has faced this exact question before, and the answers were not kind to the commanders.
On October 23, 1983, at about 6:22 in the morning, a truck bomb hit the U.S.
Marine barracks at Beirut's airport. 241 Americans died: 220 Marines, 18 sailors and 3 soldiers.
It remains one of the darkest days in Marine Corps history. Marine sentries had been under peacetime rules: weapons on safe, no round in the chamber.
Only one sentry managed to chamber a round, and by then the truck was already crashing in.
President Reagan appointed retired Admiral Robert Long to investigate. The Long Commission blamed the military chain of command for security lapses, and said fewer lives might have been lost with loaded weapons and a stronger barrier than barbed wire.
The commander on the ground was blamed. On February 7, 1984, Reagan ordered the Marines to start leaving Lebanon.
The lesson then was about walls and rules. The question is about the sky.

U.S. officials told the Financial Times they did not believe Russian intelligence was involved.
Analysts said the drone's low altitude, small size and slow speed made it hard to stop.
That is the technical answer. The harder one is who knew, and when, and it is the same question the Air Force faced in Saudi Arabia.
On June 25, 1996, a truck bomb exploded next to Building 131 at Khobar Towers in Saudi Arabia, home to U.S.
Air Force personnel. 19 airmen died and 498 people were wounded. Later reviews estimated the blast at the equivalent of 20,000 to 30,000 pounds of TNT.
In the months before, up to ten incidents of surveillance were reported from April to June.
