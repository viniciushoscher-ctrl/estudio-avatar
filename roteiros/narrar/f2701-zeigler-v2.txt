The man who spent longer on Florida's death row than anyone in the state's history has died, still insisting he was innocent.
Tommy Zeigler died in the prison hospital, his longtime attorney Terry Hadley told WESH.
He was 81. Zeigler was convicted of killing four people, including his wife, at his furniture store in Winter Garden on Christmas Eve, 1975.
When he died, his last appeal, built on new DNA tests, was still waiting at the Florida Supreme Court.
In the next minutes: what those DNA tests found, how close the state came to executing him, and what happens to the case now that he is gone.
The Tampa Bay Times reported the death first, according to the Orlando Sentinel.
WESH, citing Hadley, said Zeigler died on the night of November 24, 2026, in the prison hospital.
The Sentinel said he died on November 25, in the infirmary of the state prison.
The reports also differ on his age: 81 according to WESH and the Times, 80 according to the Sentinel.
No official cause of death was given in any report we read.
The Times reported he had been in poor health for years and needed oxygen and a wheelchair.
His lawyer was blunt. "At least he was not put to death by the state of Florida," Hadley told WESH, "and he went out on his own terms."
He also said: "this man did not get justice." And the reason his lawyers say that is a case that was still open.
At the time of his death, Zeigler was waiting for the Florida Supreme Court to decide whether new DNA testing of his clothes proved he was innocent, the Times and the Sentinel report.
Weeks before he died, the Times says, Zeigler wrote in an email that he believed the DNA testing cleared him, and that he hoped the court would order a new trial.
"I still have faith that the system will correct itself!" he wrote.
Another of his longtime lawyers, David Michaeli, told the Times he hoped to keep the appeal going.
"I think Tommy would have wanted that," he said. So the question is no longer whether Zeigler goes free.
It is whether a court will ever say who was right. And the answer depends on what the lab found.
According to the Tampa Bay Times, modern DNA tests found that Zeigler had no blood on his clothes from the relatives the state said he shot.
The Sentinel describes his lawyers' argument the same way: that blood from the victims was not on his clothes, and that this would have cleared him.
Prosecutors see it differently. According to the Sentinel, they argued that other evidence, including fingerprints and bloody clothing, would not have cleared him.
State Attorney Monique Worrell put it plainly: "The jury got it right the first time around," she said, calling him guilty beyond a reasonable doubt.
Assistant State Attorney Joshua Schow told a hearing that "the jury got it right in 1976."
Two sides, one set of test results. And a judge had already ruled on them.
In January 2025, Zeigler's attorneys filed a 64-page motion asking for a new trial, arguing the DNA results proved his innocence, according to Wikipedia, citing local reports.
In December 2025, there was a week-long evidentiary hearing in Orange County, WESH reports, on the new DNA evidence and new witness testimony.
The Times identifies the judge as Circuit Judge Leticia Marques. Then she ruled against him.
WESH and the Times say the ruling came in March 2026. The Sentinel says April.
Either way, the request for a new trial was denied, and his lawyers took it to the Florida Supreme Court.
The Sentinel adds that State Attorney Worrell had agreed to some additional DNA testing along the way.
That is where it stood when he died. To understand why so many people still argue about this case, you have to go back to one night.
On Christmas Eve, 1975, four people were killed inside Zeigler's furniture store in Winter Garden, Florida, a small town west of Orlando.
According to WESH, the victims were his wife, Eunice Zeigler, her parents, Perry and Virginia Edwards, and a man named Charlie Mays, whom the Sentinel describes as a laborer and customer.
According to Wikipedia, about 30 shots were fired. Eunice Zeigler and Virginia Edwards were shot.
Perry Edwards and Charlie Mays were beaten with a metal crank. Five guns and the crank were found at the scene.
Zeigler himself was shot in the abdomen. He was arrested a few days later, in the hospital.
And from that moment, there were two stories about what happened in that store.
The state's story, according to the Sentinel and Wikipedia, was about money.
Prosecutors said Zeigler was in debt and had bought two life insurance policies on his wife totaling 500,000 dollars.
The Sentinel says the couple had marital problems and she was thinking about separating.
Prosecutors argued he staged a robbery and shot himself to make it look like Mays and two other men were the killers.
A man named Felton Thomas testified at the trial for the state.
Zeigler's story was the opposite. He said he was the victim of a robbery gone bad and that Mays was involved, Wikipedia says.
As recently as 2015, Zeigler blamed a relative of his wife, Wikipedia says.
That man was never charged, and no one else was ever charged in the case.
And the jury had to choose between those stories.

The trial was moved to Jacksonville because of the publicity, according to Wikipedia.
On July 2, 1976, the jury convicted Zeigler. But that same jury recommended life in prison, not death.
The judge, Maurice Paul, overrode the jury and sentenced Zeigler to death on July 16, 1976.
Wikipedia reports that the death sentence covered the murders of his wife and Charlie Mays, with life sentences for the deaths of her parents.
It also notes that the judge and Zeigler had testified on opposite sides in an unrelated case months before the killings.
That override is one reason his supporters have called the case unfair for decades.
And the state did try to carry out that sentence.
With Zeigler gone, his lawyers say they want the Florida Supreme Court to finish what it started.
Michaeli told the Times he hopes to continue the appeal. The State Attorney's office has said the original verdict was right.
None of the reports we read included a statement from the victims' families, and we will not speak for them.
Four people were killed on Christmas Eve, and their families have lived with that for half a century.
Whatever the court does, it cannot give anyone those years back. And the state came closer than most people know to ending this case another way.
According to Wikipedia, Florida set an execution date for Zeigler on October 22, 1982.
A federal court in Jacksonville stayed it because of new evidence. A second date was set for May 20, 1986.
That one was stayed by the federal appeals court over the quality of his legal representation.
WESH sums it up: he escaped two death warrants. In April 1988, his death sentence was overturned, and he was sentenced to death again.
Then the long years of appeals began. WESH says he spent more time on Florida's death row than anyone in state history, and more than 50 years behind bars.
And outside the prison, his case found some famous supporters.
Human rights advocate Bianca Jagger spoke out for his innocence, the Sentinel and Wikipedia report.
According to Wikipedia, a juror who voted to convict later criticized the trial.
The case was featured on the TV show Unsolved Mysteries, in a documentary called "A Question of Innocence," and in a book called "Fatal Flaw" by Phillip Finch.
And Wikipedia reports that state witness Felton Thomas later recanted parts of his trial testimony to an investigator.
WESH's own reporter on the story, Greg Fox, has covered the case since the late 1980s.
Prosecutors have never accepted any of that as proof. And the fight over the evidence itself took decades.
According to Wikipedia, a new trial was denied in 2005, after DNA tests did not show that Charlie Mays was the killer.
Requests for more blood analysis were denied in 2013 and 2016, and a request for touch DNA testing was denied in 2017.
In 2021, a Florida prosecutor agreed to new testing, but the state attorney general moved to block it.
In July 2022, the Florida Supreme Court rejected that attempt, and testing paid for by Zeigler's lawyers was allowed.
Those are the results the two sides still argue about.
So: Tommy Zeigler, convicted of the Christmas Eve murders in Winter Garden, has died in the prison hospital after more than 50 years behind bars.
He said he was innocent to the end. Prosecutors say the jury got it right.
And his DNA appeal was still waiting at the Florida Supreme Court.
