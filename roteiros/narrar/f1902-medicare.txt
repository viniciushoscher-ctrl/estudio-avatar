On November 17, 2026, the government set the price of Medicare Part B for 2027: 209 dollars and 40 cents a month.
That is 6 dollars and 50 cents more than in 2026, or 78 dollars more over the year, according to TheStreet and Yahoo News.
For most retirees, that money comes straight out of the Social Security check, before it ever reaches the bank.
And it lands in the same month as the 3.6 percent raise.
So the question every retiree is asking: how much of the raise is really yours?
In the next minutes: the exact numbers for the average retiree, a couple, a widow and a disabled worker, the rule that protects some of you, the bigger bills for others, and what Medicare cost when it began.
Here is what the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services announced. The standard Part B premium rises to 209 dollars and 40 cents a month in 2027, up 6 dollars and 50 cents, or about 3.2 percent, TheStreet reports.
The yearly Part B deductible rises 9 dollars, to 292 dollars. The Part A hospital deductible rises 40 dollars, to 1,776 dollars for each benefit period.
CMS said the increase is "mainly due to projected increases in healthcare spending," according to Yahoo News.
About 99 percent of people on Medicare pay no premium for Part A, TheStreet reports, because they or a spouse paid Medicare taxes long enough.
So for most of you, Part B is the number that matters.
And Part B is paid out of your Social Security.
The raise came first. On October 14, the Social Security Administration announced a 3.6 percent cost-of-living adjustment for January 2027, according to 24/7 Wall St.
It covers about 76 million Americans. It is the largest increase in several years.
The 2026 raise was 2.8 percent. For the average retired worker, TheStreet calculates the monthly check rises from 2,095 dollars to 2,170 dollars.
That is 75 dollars more a month. For an average retired couple who both collect, the check goes from 3,238 dollars to 3,355 dollars.
On paper, that is a solid raise. But the check you see in January will not show the full 75 dollars.
Take the 75 dollar raise. Subtract the 6 dollars and 50 cents Medicare increase.
The average retired worker keeps about 69 dollars a month, TheStreet reports; Yahoo News puts it at about 68 dollars and 50 cents.
That means Medicare takes about 8.7 percent of the average retiree's raise.
For disabled workers, it is worse: about 11 percent of a 59 dollar raise.
For aged widows and widowers, about 9.4 percent of a 69 dollar raise, according to TheStreet.
You can run your own number the same way. If your check is 1,500 dollars, a 3.6 percent raise adds 54 dollars; minus 6 dollars and 50 cents, you keep 47 dollars and 50 cents.
If your check is 3,000 dollars, the raise is 108 dollars, and you keep 101 dollars and 50 cents.
The smaller your check, the bigger the share Medicare takes. Over a year, that is 78 dollars that never reaches your bank account.
And that is only the standard premium. Some retirees will pay far more.
If your income is higher, Medicare charges a surcharge called IRMAA. In 2027, it starts above 112,000 dollars of income for a single person, and above 224,000 dollars for a married couple, Yahoo News reports.
About 9 percent of Part B enrollees pay it. At the top, people with more than 500,000 dollars of income, or 750,000 dollars for couples, pay 711 dollars and 80 cents a month for Part B.
There are also surcharges on Part D drug plans, from 15 dollars and 40 cents to 96 dollars and 40 cents a month on top of the plan's premium.
One detail many people miss: Medicare looks back two years. Your 2027 premium is set using your 2025 income, according to 24/7 Wall St.
So a one-time event, like selling a house, can raise your bill two years later.
There is an appeal form for life-changing events, like retirement or the death of a spouse.
There is a rule called "hold harmless." It says your Part B increase cannot be larger than the dollar amount of your Social Security raise, so your net check does not go down, TheStreet reports.
If your raise is smaller than 6 dollars and 50 cents, your premium increase is capped.
But the rule does not cover everyone. According to 24/7 Wall St, it does not protect new enrollees, people who pay their premium directly instead of through Social Security, or higher-income people who pay IRMAA.
And with a raise as big as 3.6 percent, the rule will rarely kick in this time.
So most retirees will simply pay the full 209 dollars and 40 cents.
That premium has climbed fast.
In 2025, the standard Part B premium was 185 dollars a month.
In 2026, it jumped to 202 dollars and 90 cents, an increase of about 9.7 percent, 24/7 Wall St reports.
It reaches 209 dollars and 40 cents. That is about 24 dollars and 40 cents more a month in two years, or nearly 293 dollars more a year.
Medicare's own trustees had projected 209 dollars and 50 cents for 2027 in their report.
The final number came in ten cents lower. Shannon Benton, executive director of The Senior Citizens League, put it this way: "Medicare Part B premiums consistently overtaking Social Security COLAs degrades American seniors' quality of life over time," as quoted by TheStreet.
Supporters of the current system point out that the raise is bigger than the premium increase, so the average retiree still comes out ahead.
And to see how far we have come, you have to go back to the start.

On July 30, 1965, President Lyndon Johnson signed Medicare into law in Independence, Missouri, at the library of former President Harry Truman.
Truman, who had pushed for national health insurance twenty years earlier, received the first Medicare card.
Coverage began in July 1966. The monthly premium for what we call Part B was 3 dollars, according to the Social Security Administration's history.
For 2027 it is 209 dollars and 40 cents. Of course, prices and wages have risen a lot since 1966, and Medicare covers much more care than it did then.
But for a retiree on a fixed income, the real question is not 1966.
It is next month, and the deductible that comes with it.
On top of the premium, you pay deductibles before Medicare pays its share.
In 2027, the Part B deductible is 292 dollars for the year, up 9 dollars.
If you go into the hospital, the Part A deductible is 1,776 dollars for each benefit period, up 40 dollars, TheStreet and Yahoo News report.
A benefit period can restart after you have been out of the hospital for a while, so some people pay it more than once in a year.
For the small number who must buy Part A, the full premium is 597 dollars a month, up from 565 dollars; people with at least 30 quarters of work pay a reduced 328 dollars, Yahoo News reports.
These are the numbers to write on your calendar for January. About that raise: there have been far bigger ones.
Social Security raises were not automatic until 1975. Before that, Congress had to vote for each increase, according to the Social Security Administration.
The first automatic raise was 8 percent. The biggest one came in 1980: 14.3 percent, as inflation hit double digits under President Carter.
And there have been years with nothing at all. In the agency's table, the raise listed for 2010 is zero.
More recently, retirees got 8.7 percent, paid in January 2023, after prices jumped.
Compared with those years, 3.6 percent is middle of the road. What matters is what happens to prices next.
Here is what to check. First, your Social Security letter. It usually arrives in December and online in your my Social Security account, and it shows your new benefit and the Medicare amount taken out.
Second, if you pay IRMAA and your income dropped because you retired or lost a spouse, ask about the form for a life-changing event.
Third, open enrollment for Medicare Advantage and Part D drug plans runs every year from October 15 to December 7, so there are still a few weeks to compare plans.
Fourth, watch out for scammers. They use news like this to call seniors about a "new Medicare card" or a "premium refund."
Medicare does not call you to ask for your number.
So here is where it stands. Medicare Part B rises to 209 dollars and 40 cents in January.
Social Security rises 3.6 percent. The average retiree gains about 75 dollars and keeps about 69 dollars after the premium increase.
An average couple gains about 117 dollars before two premium increases of 6 dollars and 50 cents each.
The new amounts show up in January checks. Higher-income retirees will get IRMAA notices.
And the 2027 trustees report will give the first estimate for 2028.
