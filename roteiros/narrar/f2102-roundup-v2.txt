A Missouri judge has given final approval to a settlement of up to 7.25 billion dollars for people who say Roundup weed killer gave them cancer.
The Associated Press reports that Judge Timothy Boyer of the St. Louis Circuit Court signed the judgment on November 20, 2026.
The money goes to people with non-Hodgkin lymphoma, a blood cancer. Individual payments run from 10,000 dollars to 165,000 dollars.
And it does not only cover people who are sick. It also covers people who could be diagnosed years from now.

Judge Boyer wrote that the payments are "fair and adequate," according to the AP.
He also wrote that the total is "among the largest in the history of mass tort personal-injury class action settlements."
The money will not arrive in one lump. The AP reports it will be paid in yearly installments into a special fund, for up to 21 years.
Agri-Pulse reports the judge rejected numerous objections and found no evidence of collusion between the company and the lawyers who negotiated the deal.
Separately, the AP reports, 600 million dollars is set aside for attorneys' fees.
That is money that does not go to patients. So who actually gets paid?
According to Agri-Pulse, there are two groups in the settlement. The first group: people who were exposed to Roundup before February 17, 2026, and who already have a non-Hodgkin lymphoma diagnosis.
The second group: people exposed before that date who are diagnosed within a 16-year window after the settlement's final approval.
That second group is the unusual part. It means a man who sprayed Roundup on his fence line for 20 summers, and is perfectly healthy, may already be inside this settlement without knowing it.
Agri-Pulse reports the court's order guarantees 3 billion dollars of funding for the first group over the first 5 years, with faster payment options.
Now, how much does each person get?
The AP reports that payments depend on three things: how the product was used, how old the person was when diagnosed, and how severe the cancer is.
The AP gives two examples. A farm, industrial or turf worker with long exposure, who was diagnosed with an aggressive form of the cancer before age 60, could average 165,000 dollars.
Someone diagnosed at 78 or older could average 10,000 dollars. That is a difference of more than 16 times.
For many of the people watching this channel, that second number is the one that matters.
The older you were at diagnosis, the smaller the check, under this formula.
And that is exactly where the critics start.
Not every lawyer likes this deal. The AP reports that some plaintiffs' attorneys think the payouts are too small.
At a hearing in September, attorney Gerson Smoger called it "the scariest, worst future settlement that's ever been contemplated," the AP reports.
His concern, and others', is the future group: people who are not sick yet are being folded into a deal, with amounts set in advance, for a diagnosis that may come 10 or 15 years later.
On the other side, Christopher Seeger, the court-appointed lawyer for cancer patients, said the order "delivers guaranteed compensation to Roundup cancer victims."
Guaranteed is the key word for supporters. Because the other road, a jury trial, is no longer what it was.
How big is this? The AP reports that about 200,000 Roundup claims have been made in total.
Judge Boyer's order, as reported by Agri-Pulse, says there are more than 30,000 cases in Missouri courts alone, and tens of thousands more pending elsewhere or paused under agreements.
Just over 200 plaintiffs had trial dates set. The AP reports that Bayer had already set aside 16 billion dollars for Roundup legal costs, and this settlement comes on top of that.
Bayer's chief executive, Bill Anderson, called the approval "a decisive milestone in the company's strategy to mitigate the risks of both pending and future Roundup litigation," the AP and Agri-Pulse report.
To understand why Bayer wanted this, you need to know what the Supreme Court did in June.
In June 2026, the Supreme Court decided a case called Durnell, 7 votes to 2, Agri-Pulse reports.
The court held that federal pesticide law blocks state-court claims that the company failed to warn about cancer.
The AP puts it plainly: Bayer cannot be sued in state courts on claims it failed to warn about cancer.
Judge Boyer wrote that failure-to-warn claims "were the principal legal theory on which Roundup litigation has been based."
In other words, the main legal weapon plaintiffs used for years was taken away.
Bayer itself says the settlement and the Durnell win are "mutually reinforcing pillars" of its strategy, according to Agri-Pulse.
For people with lymphoma, the settlement may be the surest path to any money at all.
Before the ruling, juries were split. The AP reports that only a couple dozen Roundup cases ever went to a jury, with mixed results.
One Georgia jury awarded 2.1 billion dollars. But in October 2026, a Missouri jury sided with Bayer in a case over design defect, negligence and fraud.
Bill Dodero, Bayer's U.S. general counsel, said that verdict "should send a strong signal," the AP reports.
With 2 or 3 big wins on each side, neither the company nor the patients could count on a jury.
That uncertainty is what produced a deal. And a deal of this size has deep roots.

Roundup went on sale in 1974, the AP reports, with its key ingredient, glyphosate.
For decades, it was the weed killer in millions of American garages and on farms across the Midwest.
Bayer, the German company, bought Roundup's maker, Monsanto, in 2018 for 63 billion dollars, Agri-Pulse reports.
With that purchase, Bayer also bought the lawsuits. Many men in their 70s or 80s remember Roundup the way they remember a favorite pocketknife: always on the shelf, always in use.
That is why this settlement reaches so deep into rural America. But is the chemical actually dangerous?
That depends on who you ask.
In 2015, the World Health Organization's cancer research agency classified glyphosate as "probably carcinogenic," the AP reports.
The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency disagrees. The EPA says glyphosate is "not likely to cause cancer in humans when used as directed," according to the AP.
Bayer has always maintained that Roundup is safe. Still, the AP reports, Bayer removed glyphosate from new versions of Roundup sold for home lawns and gardens.
So the bottle on a store shelf may not contain the same ingredient as the bottle your father bought.
A settlement is not an admission, and the science fight will go on for 1 more generation at least.
Look again at the 2 examples the AP gave, because they say a lot about who this deal favors.
A younger worker with heavy exposure and an aggressive cancer before 60: about 165,000 dollars on average.
A person diagnosed at 78 or older: about 10,000 dollars on average.
Non-Hodgkin lymphoma is a cancer that is often diagnosed later in life.
That means many of the people who used Roundup the longest, the farmers and groundskeepers who are in their 70s and 80s, sit near the bottom of the scale.
One way to read the formula: a younger patient loses more working years.
Another way: older Americans who sprayed this product for 40 years get far less for the same illness.
Either way, under this formula, age changes the check more than almost anything else.
And for the future group, every year of waiting moves a healthy person further down that scale.
Based on the terms reported by the AP and Agri-Pulse, 3 kinds of people should pay close attention.
First, anyone who used Roundup before February 17, 2026, and has non-Hodgkin lymphoma.
Second, the families of those people, since the settlement terms and the claims process will matter to them.
Third, anyone who used Roundup regularly and is healthy, because the future group runs for 16 years.
Neither the AP nor Agri-Pulse described the step-by-step claims process. If this applies to you, the sensible move is to speak with a lawyer you trust, or to look up the official settlement notice by name, and to keep any records of where and how long you used the product.
So here is where it stands. A Missouri judge has approved Bayer's Roundup settlement of up to 7.25 billion dollars, paid over as long as 21 years.
Payments range from 10,000 to 165,000 dollars, and older patients get less.
The AP reports that the decision could be appealed. That matters: until any appeals are resolved, the timing of payments could change.
Bayer will make its yearly payments into the fund, and lawyers on both sides will keep fighting over the people who opted out or never signed up.
For the roughly 200,000 claims the AP counted, the question is simple: how soon does the first check arrive, and how big will it be?
