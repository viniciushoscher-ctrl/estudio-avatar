The general who ran plans and policy for the Joint Chiefs of Staff could now face a court-martial, The Washington Post reported on November 15, 2026.
He is Army Lieutenant General Brett Sylvia, 54. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth fired him on October 29 over alleged leaks of classified information to reporters.
In the next minutes: what the Pentagon says he did, what he could lose, and how it ended for two four-star generals who fell over secrets before him.
The administration has directed the military to explore its own criminal case against Sylvia, The Washington Post reported, according to Newsmax.
The decision was delegated to the acting Army Secretary, Adam Telle.
That is on top of a referral Hegseth's team already sent to the Justice Department.
The Justice Department says it has "ongoing investigations related to possible leaks of classified information."
At the time of this report, no military or federal charges had been filed, and Sylvia had not spoken publicly.
So who is the man at the center of this?
Sylvia led strategy, plans and policy for the Joint Staff, the post known as J5, and reported to General Dan Caine, chairman of the Joint Chiefs, Newsmax reported.
He had held that job for 11 months, ABC News reported, after serving as vice director of the same office, the AP reported.
CBS News reported he was coordinating the Iran battle plan when he was fired. A Pentagon official told the AP he was not a "central planner" on Iran, a role that belongs to U.S. Central Command.
He commanded the 101st Airborne Division at Fort Campbell, Kentucky, until May 2025. He deployed several times to Afghanistan and Iraq, earned six Bronze Star Medals, one for valor, and received a Purple Heart after being wounded in a gunfight with the Taliban, CBS and the AP reported.
He served 32 years in the Army. Back home in South Carolina's Lowcountry, The Post and Courier remembered him as a standout student-athlete.
So what is he accused of?
A Pentagon official told CBS News that Sylvia was accused of leaking classified information about the Iran war and the Russia-Ukraine war.
Axios, citing an internal Pentagon report, said Sylvia admitted leaking classified information about Ukraine and Iran to two reporters from separate outlets.
The Washington Post's sources said he acknowledged taking notes from material classified as secret, passing information from them to a reporter, and deleting a Signal chat.
The Post said it could not independently verify those claims, and the Pentagon declined to share its investigation.
The timing matters. The firing came as President Trump weighed renewed strikes on Iran, in a war then eight months old, the AP reported.
And the information he allegedly shared touched both of America's hottest fronts: Iran and Ukraine.
But not everyone inside the Pentagon agrees with the accusation. Who disagrees?
Two other Pentagon sources told CBS News the firing was related to polygraph tests, but that the accusations are not true.
The polygraph count is not settled either. A Pentagon official told CBS he failed three. Newsmax reported four.
And a retired Army lawyer, Franklin Rosenblatt, said the emphasis on polygraphs is "problematic," because polygraph results are not admissible in courts-martial.
Sylvia has not commented, and he did not respond to the AP. No lawyer has spoken for him.
And one source gave ABC News a vaguer reason for his removal: a "lack of confidence."
So what did the Pentagon say out loud?
On October 29, Pentagon chief spokesman Sean Parnell posted on X: "We vowed to find you, and we meant it," Newsmax reported.
Parnell's official statement did not name Sylvia. It said "following an extensive Air Force OSI investigation, the Department took necessary personnel action," and added: "Classified information is not personal property," CBS reported.
President Trump told reporters the same day: "We're very much looking for leakers."
A senior Pentagon official told ABC News that Hegseth fired Sylvia for "severe misconduct" and took "immediate action." Parnell said the Pentagon has "zero tolerance" for unauthorized disclosure of classified information.
Then the Joint Staff did something unusual. What?
The Joint Staff first issued a statement praising Sylvia's 32 years of service, CBS News reported.
Then it pulled it back. The new statement said the first one "did not appropriately reflect the full circumstances."
Sylvia is the second general removed from the J5 post during Hegseth's tenure. His predecessor, Lieutenant General Joseph McGee, was forced to retire in October 2025, CBS reported, after he was not renominated, according to ABC News.
No replacement for Sylvia has been named.
And it is not the first leak hunt under Hegseth. Is it?
Hegseth fired three senior aides last year in a broader leak investigation that included polygraphs, the AP reported.
Those aides protested, and no details of their alleged conduct were ever made public.
The shake-up at the top has been wide. Army Chief of Staff General Randy George was ousted by Hegseth, ABC News noted, and Army Secretary Dan Driscoll resigned amid tensions with him.
The Director of the Joint Staff, Lieutenant General Douglas Sims, also retired early, ABC reported.
So when a general is the target, what would a military trial actually look like?
A military case would first need an Article 32 preliminary hearing before any general court-martial, Newsmax reported.
Yale Law School's Eugene Fidell said a panel of eight higher-ranking officers would probably decide the case.
Rosenblatt said Sylvia's defense would "almost certainly" raise unlawful command influence, because of the public statements from the top.
Newsmax did not list possible penalties, and no charge has been filed.
So what does Sylvia stand to lose?
He has already lost his post at the top of the Joint Staff, one of the most senior planning jobs in the military.
His name now sits in a Justice Department referral and a possible Army case.
Either way, the Pentagon has already put his case in public, with a spokesman's post, a withdrawn statement and a Justice Department referral.
If no charge comes, he still retires under a public cloud after 32 years, a Purple Heart and command of the 101st Airborne.
If a charge does come, eight officers senior to him could decide his fate. Has that happened to a famous general before?
Yes, and Americans remember him.
David Petraeus, the four-star who led the surge in Iraq, resigned as CIA Director on November 9, 2012, citing an affair with his biographer.
Investigators found he had given her classified information, including top secret material.
On March 3, 2015, he pleaded guilty in Charlotte, North Carolina, to one misdemeanor count of mishandling classified information.
On April 23, 2015, he was sentenced to two years of probation and a 100,000 dollar fine. No prison.
And by May 2013, the private equity firm KKR had already hired him to lead a new institute.
So a plea and a fine. Is that how leak cases usually end?
Petraeus was retired and a civilian CIA director when he was charged. Sylvia was a serving officer when he was fired, which is why the Army can consider its own case.
Petraeus was charged in a federal civilian court. A court-martial is a military court, with a panel of officers instead of a civilian jury.
And the political moment is different too. Congress changes hands in January, with Democrats in control of both chambers.
So which way could a case go? The next general shows the other ending.
