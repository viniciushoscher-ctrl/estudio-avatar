Pete Rose, baseball's all-time hit king, has been pardoned. On November 19, 2026, President Trump wrote that he had given "a Complete and Total Pardon to the Late, Great Pete Rose, a true Baseball SUPERSTAR & LEGEND," according to the Associated Press.
Rose collected 4,256 hits, more than anyone who ever played the game.
The pardon covers the federal tax crime that sent him to a prison camp for five months.
It comes more than two years after his death, at 83. In the next minutes: exactly what this pardon erases, whether it puts him in the Hall of Fame, and what Rose said on the radio the night a judge sentenced him.
The president announced it on social media, the AP reports, and gave no date for a signing.
Rose died in 2024, the BBC reports, and Major League Baseball had reinstated him one year before this pardon.
Trump wrote that Rose "was treated terribly by Major League Baseball in not allowing him to go into the Baseball Hall of Fame.
Politics, I guess," according to the BBC. He also wrote that Rose's "record of 4,256 Hits in a Season will never be broken."
That number is Rose's career total, not a single season. The BBC calls the pardon "largely symbolic."
It notes that the Justice Department typically does not process applications for pardons after someone has died.
The BBC said it asked the Hall of Fame and the Justice Department for comment and reported no reply.
So what crime did the president actually wipe away?
It is not the gambling. It is taxes. In April 1990, Rose pleaded guilty in Cincinnati to two counts of filing false tax returns, the AP reports.
He had failed to report 354,968 dollars of income over four years.
That money came largely from autograph signings, card shows and memorabilia, the kind of cash the hit king could collect at any weekend show.
He faced up to six years in prison and 500,000 dollars in fines.
In the end, Rose repaid 366,042 dollars to the Internal Revenue Service, according to the AP.
The pardon forgives that federal conviction. It does not return the money and it does not change baseball's records.
And the sentence he received surprised many of his fans.
That July, U.S. District Judge S. Arthur Spiegel sentenced Rose to five months in prison, a 50,000 dollar fine, and 1,000 hours of community service, the AP reports.
The community service was as a gym teacher's assistant, working with inner-city kids.
After prison came a year of probation, with the first three months in a halfway house.
In court, the judge said: "History and the tincture of time will decide his place among the all-time greats of baseball."
That night, Rose spoke on his own radio show on WCKY-AM. "I know I'm not a bad person, but I did make some mistakes and it's in the eyes of the judge," he said, according to the AP.
The question is whether the pardon moves history's verdict at all.
No, not by itself. The Hall of Fame's own board rule bars anyone on baseball's permanently ineligible list from being elected, the AP reports, and the pardon does not change Rose's Hall of Fame status, the BBC reports.
What changed his status was baseball itself. In May 2025, Commissioner Rob Manfred ruled that people on the permanently ineligible list have their status restored when they die.
ESPN reports it covered 17 people; the BBC says Rose and 16 others.
That made Rose eligible again, more than two decades after his ban.
Rose asked to be reinstated more than once while he was alive, and he was never let back in.
Only his death changed his status. The earliest Rose could be inducted is 2028, according to the AP.
So the pardon is about his name, not his plaque. And his name is attached to numbers nobody has come close to.
Rose finished with 4,256 hits. He passed Ty Cobb's record of 4,191, the AP reports.
He played 3,562 games, according to the BBC. He was a 17-time All-Star, a three-time National League batting champion, Rookie of the Year in his first season, the league's Most Valuable Player, and World Series MVP.
He spent five seasons with the Philadelphia Phillies and won a World Series ring there too, the AP reports.
Before that, he was the heart of Cincinnati's Big Red Machine. They called him "Charlie Hustle" because he ran to first base even after a walk.
No active player is anywhere near his hit total. But all those numbers were frozen out of Cooperstown for one reason.
Trump first promised this pardon in March 2025. In a post at the time, he wrote that "Major League Baseball didn't have the courage or decency to put the late, great, Pete Rose" into the Hall of Fame, according to the BBC.
Supporters of Rose say he paid for his crimes long ago, served his time, repaid the IRS, and that his playing record is clean.
Critics answer that the pardon covers taxes, not baseball's real charge against him, which was betting.
Baseball's rule against gambling is posted in every clubhouse, and Rose broke it.
That is why, even with a pardon in hand, the Hall of Fame vote will still be a fight.
To understand that fight, you have to go back to the summer of his ban.

In August 1989, Major League Baseball banned Pete Rose for life. The decision followed an investigation by MLB's investigator, John M.
Dowd, who concluded that Rose bet on the Cincinnati Reds while he was playing for and managing the team, from 1985 to 1987, according to the AP.
Rose was the Reds' manager when the report came out. Eight months after the ban, he pleaded guilty in the tax case.
For the men who grew up watching him dive headfirst into second base, it happened almost overnight: the hit king, banned from the game, and then headed to federal prison.
Where he served that time says a lot about how far he fell.
On August 10, Rose reported to the prison camp at the U.S.
Penitentiary in Marion, Illinois, a minimum-security camp, the AP reports. The most hits in baseball history, and later an inmate number.
He was released on January 7, 1991. Then he went to Talbert House, a halfway house in Cincinnati, and left on April 5.
He did his 1,000 hours of community service with kids in the city where he was born and where he became famous.
For the rest of his life, he signed autographs, made appearances, and kept asking to be let back in.
He never got the answer he wanted while he was alive. He is not the first legend to wait that long.
Shoeless Joe Jackson was banned after the 1919 World Series, when members of the Chicago White Sox were accused of fixing games.
A jury acquitted the players in 1921, but baseball's first commissioner, Kenesaw Mountain Landis, banned them anyway.
Jackson was among those restored by Manfred's 2025 ruling, ESPN reports. And Rose is not the first sports legend Trump has pardoned after death.
In 2018, Trump posthumously pardoned Jack Johnson, the first Black heavyweight boxing champion, who had been convicted in 1913 under a law used against him because of his relationship with a white woman.
Sylvester Stallone stood beside the president in the Oval Office that day.
Rose joins that short list.
The Constitution gives the president the power to pardon federal crimes, and that power has no deadline: it can be used after a person has died, as it was here.
The president does not need the Justice Department's approval, and Congress cannot overturn it.
A presidential pardon forgives a federal crime. It can restore a person's name in the eyes of the law.
It does not erase a guilty plea from history, it does not refund the 50,000 dollar fine, and it does not bind the Hall of Fame.
Baseball's 2025 ruling already took care of his eligibility. So in practical terms, the pardon is a statement: the president of the United States believes Pete Rose was treated unfairly.
For his fans, that may matter more than paper. Many of them were in the stands in Cincinnati and Philadelphia, and they never stopped wearing his number.
For the people who think the gambling ban was right, the pardon changes nothing, because the tax case was never the reason he was kept out.
For the voters who will decide on Cooperstown, it is one more argument on the table.
So here is where it stands. Pete Rose has a full presidential pardon for his federal tax conviction.
Baseball restored his eligibility in 2025. The Hall of Fame vote is still ahead, and the earliest induction is 2028.
