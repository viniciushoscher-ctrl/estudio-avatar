A five-term congressman from Los Angeles is leaving the House. Democratic Representative Jimmy Gomez announced on November 23, 2026, that he would resign effective December 1.
He did it the same day the House Ethics Committee released a report saying it found substantial evidence of sexual misconduct toward a House staffer.
Gomez disputes the report's conclusions, and he has not been charged with any crime.
In the next minutes: what the committee found, what Gomez says in his defense, and how it ended for the senators and congressmen who left Washington the same way.
According to the Associated Press, Gomez announced on November 23 in a written statement that he would resign.
His resignation takes effect December 1. He has represented California's 34th Congressional District, in Los Angeles, for five terms.
Newsweek and PJ Media report he chose that date so his staff could finish casework for constituents.
CBS reports the House Ethics Committee released its report the same day.
The timing matters for another reason. Gomez had already lost his reelection race earlier in November to a fellow Democrat, Angela Gonzales-Torres, under California's top-two system, the AP and Newsweek report.
His term was going to end in January anyway. Instead, he leaves a month early, and with a report on the public record.
What the report says is the heart of this story.
CBS reports the committee found substantial evidence of sexual misconduct, including unwanted sexual advances and sexual contact with a House staffer.
The AP says the committee found his interactions violated House rules by making unwelcome sexual advances.
The report says, according to CBS, that "Representative Gomez failed in his obligation to behave in a manner that reflects creditably on the House."
It says the conduct caused the staffer significant and ongoing distress and contributed to her leaving her job.
No criminal charge or referral to prosecutors has been reported. And Gomez is telling a very different story.
In his statement, Gomez said he has "strong disagreements with the Committee's report and its conclusions," the AP reports.
He said he was selectively quoted and disregarded in the final report and given no opportunity to respond.
He also said he recognizes the seriousness of the report, and that he takes full responsibility for what he called personal mistakes outside of his marriage, according to PJ Media.
He apologized to his family and his constituents, the AP reports, and said he cooperated with the investigation.
In August 2026, CBS reports, he said his actions were consensual. PJ Media reports he said he saw no other alternative than to resign.
So he is leaving while still disputing the findings. And the findings describe two specific moments.
According to CBS and Newsweek, the report centers on one woman, a House staffer, and two incidents in 2024.
Newsweek and PJ Media report the first was in February at a bar in Washington.
PJ Media reports the staffer said he touched her under her skirt, and that Gomez later admitted putting his hand beneath her skirt but denied anything further and said he asked permission.
Newsweek reports the second incident was in July in his congressional office, where she said he grabbed her and tried to kiss her over her objections.
He said he hugged her and kissed her on the cheek. We are not naming the staffer.
Her lawyers did speak publicly, though.
The staffer's attorneys, Lisa Banks and Sarah Nesbitt, said Gomez "abused the power given to him by the voters," according to the AP and Newsweek.
They praised the Ethics Committee and called on Congress to deal with what they described as a toxic and predatory culture.
The AP reports the committee also interviewed a second staffer, after Gomez disclosed a past consensual relationship that ended in 2022.
She denied to the committee any nonconsensual encounter with him. So the case rests on one accuser and two incidents.
And the people who run his party reacted within hours.
House Democratic leaders Hakeem Jeffries, Katherine Clark and Pete Aguilar issued a joint statement calling the findings "serious and deeply troubling," according to PJ Media.
"It is appropriate that he is stepping down," they said. They added that decisive action to set a new standard for reform is an urgent priority for House Democrats.
The chair of the Democratic Women's Caucus, Teresa Leger Fernandez, called his actions "reprehensible and a gross abuse of power," Newsweek reports.
No statement from Speaker Mike Johnson was found in the reports we read.
So what does Gomez actually lose by leaving a month early?
On paper, not much time. His term would have ended on January 3, 2027, when Gonzales-Torres is sworn in, according to Newsweek.
But the AP reports the committee did not recommend any particular sanction.
PJ Media reports it declined to recommend censure or expulsion because Gomez is leaving before that process could finish.
The committee wrote, according to the AP, that "the House should take whatever action it deems appropriate."
By resigning, he avoids a possible House vote on punishment. What he cannot avoid is the report itself, which stays public.
Newsweek reports Governor Gavin Newsom had not said whether he would call a special election, and the seat was likely to stay empty until January 2027.
Washington has seen this kind of exit before.
Before we look at the men who left Congress the same way, tell us in the comments: should a member of Congress found by the Ethics Committee to have done this be allowed to stay until the end of the term, or should the House act right away?
We read every answer.
Many of you remember Bob Packwood, the Oregon Republican. In November 1992, the Washington Post reported claims of sexual abuse and assault from 10 women, according to Wikipedia; eventually 19 women came forward.
On September 7, 1995, the Senate Ethics Committee unanimously recommended that he be expelled.
The next morning, he announced his resignation, saying he was aware of the dishonor that had befallen him.
He had served in the Senate since 1969 and left on October 1, 1995.
He then started a lobbying firm, which Wikipedia says earned him more than 440,000 dollars in 1998.
Packwood's case took three years. Gomez's took months. And the next case came from his own party.
The speed is the change. Gomez's case, from the first public report to resignation, took a matter of months, according to CBS and Newsweek, which date the start of the investigation to the spring and summer of 2026.
The House Democratic leaders' statement talks about a "new standard." And the staffer's lawyers want Congress to change its culture, not just lose one member.
For a 65-plus viewer, the pattern is familiar. Each generation of scandals has pushed Congress to move faster.
The question is whether faster also means fairer for everyone involved, including the accused, who in this case says he was never allowed to respond.
The next precedent shows how fast it can go.
John Conyers of Michigan was the longest-serving member of Congress at the time, the dean of the House, according to Wikipedia.
He had served since 1965. In November 2017, BuzzFeed reported allegations that he had sexually harassed female staff members and secretly used taxpayer money to settle a harassment claim.
The House Ethics Committee opened an investigation the next day. On December 5, 2017, at age 88, he resigned.
More than 50 years in Congress ended in about two weeks. A Senator from Minnesota was about to follow him out the door.
There is one big difference between those cases and this one. Packwood faced a committee vote to expel him.
Conyers resigned before the Ethics Committee finished. Gomez resigned after the committee finished and published its findings, while still disputing them.
He had also already lost his election, which is why the AP reports the committee said there was not enough time for a House sanction.
That leaves the report as the final word from Congress, unless the House decides to act in the days before December 1.
Which brings us to the senator who resigned without any ethics finding at all.
On November 16, 2017, broadcaster Leeann Tweeden alleged that Senator Al Franken of Minnesota had kissed her without consent during a 2006 USO tour rehearsal, according to Wikipedia.
More women came forward in the following days. More than two dozen Democratic senators called on him to resign.
Franken announced on December 7, 2017 that he would resign, and he left the Senate on January 2, 2018, before any Senate ethics review was completed.
That debate, over process and fairness, is the one Gomez is raising about his own case.
