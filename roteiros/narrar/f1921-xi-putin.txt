For the first time ever, the leaders of the United States, China and Russia sat down together, and they signed one sentence: "A nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought."
The meeting took place on November 19, 2026, in Shenzhen, China, at the Asia-Pacific summit.
According to the AP and the German news agency DPA, the three countries agreed to a dialogue on reducing nuclear risks, to push for reforms at the United Nations, and to boost oil supplies.
That sentence is not new. Ronald Reagan and Mikhail Gorbachev put the same words in their joint statement in Geneva, almost exactly 41 years ago.
In the next minutes: the four points they signed, the two wars they left out, and what Putin told Xi as they walked out of the room.
The joint declaration has four points, DPA reports. First, a dialogue on reducing nuclear risks and preventing the spread of nuclear weapons.
Second, reform of the United Nations "to more effectively address global challenges," in the AP's wording.
Third, more production and supply of oil products, for what the leaders called "stable energy flows."
Fourth, a line on international courts: the International Criminal Court and similar bodies must not abuse their jurisdiction, and state officials should have immunity, the AP reports.
That fourth point matters because Vladimir Putin is wanted by the International Criminal Court over suspected war crimes in Ukraine.
So one of the four points protects one of the three men at the table.
And what is missing from the paper may be bigger than what is on it.
The declaration contains no specific disarmament commitments and no timetable, DPA reports.
It does not mention the war in Ukraine. It does not mention the war with Iran.
President Trump said he raised both earlier in the day, according to the AP.
And the last treaty that capped American and Russian nuclear arsenals, New START, lapsed in February, Al Jazeera reports.
So at the time of this report, there was no treaty limiting the two biggest nuclear arsenals on earth.
A promise to "talk" is not a limit. Analysts told Al Jazeera before the meeting that Xi is reluctant to join a nuclear treaty China did not help write.
So the declaration may be as far as Beijing would go at that point.
But the words the leaders used in the room went further than the paper did.
Trump, speaking about nuclear weapons, said, "We are really making history," and added that it is "not an easy thing to do — give them up," Reuters reports.
Xi went further. According to China's Xinhua agency, as reported by Reuters, he said nuclear weapons should be "prohibited" and "eliminated."
Xi called the meeting "fruitful" and said, "As long as dialogue does not stop, doors remain open, there is hope for peace," the AP reports.
Putin called the talks "open and candid" and "highly productive." He said he had no doubt Trump genuinely wants to resolve conflicts, including Ukraine.
Then, as they parted, Putin told Xi, "I don't think anyone even expected things to go so well."
The Kremlin released video of Xi walking Putin to his car. Those pictures will be shown in Moscow for weeks.
But for American families, the oil line may matter most.
The three leaders pledged to increase production and supply of oil products for "stable energy flows."
Trump said he and Xi both feel strongly that oil must flow through the Strait of Hormuz, Reuters reports.
That waterway has been disrupted by the war with Iran. Talks between Washington and Tehran are deadlocked, Al Jazeera reports, over whether to deal first with Hormuz and money, or with Iran's nuclear program.
Observers call it a "frozen war." Meanwhile, Russia has been selling more oil to China: Russian oil exports to China rose 35 percent in the first quarter of this year, according to a Kremlin aide cited by Al Jazeera.
If more oil flows, prices at the pump could ease. If not, the pledge stays on paper.
And not everyone at the summit believed it would amount to much.
France's foreign minister, Jean-Noël Barrot, was blunt. "These three leaders agree on nothing, so, in a sense, nothing is going to come out of this meeting," he said, according to the AP.
Ruby Osman of the Tony Blair Institute said Beijing "wanted to be seen as the adult in the room," and that real progress would likely come in one-on-one meetings.
There was also a fight over the press. A Bloomberg reporter, Annmarie Hordern, was barred from the three-way meeting after asking a question during Trump's earlier meeting with Xi.
A White House official said the Chinese insisted she be kept out, despite White House pressure, the AP reports.
So the critics see a photo opportunity. Supporters see a first step.
And the first step sounds familiar for a reason.
Before the meeting, analysts told Al Jazeera what each leader was after.
Trump wanted a "win" he could show at home, after Republicans lost ground in the midterms and the Iran war hurt his standing.
Xi wanted no return to the tariff war, no pushback on Taiwan, and room for China's economy.
US tariffs on Chinese goods once peaked at 145 percent, and China's on American goods at 125 percent.
Putin wanted to end Russia's isolation and to sit with the United States as a near-equal.
By that measure, Putin may have gotten the most: the photo, the ICC line, and a statement that never mentions Ukraine.
Trump got a historic first and a nuclear sentence. Xi got to host.

In November 1985, Ronald Reagan met Mikhail Gorbachev in Geneva. It was the first U.S.-Soviet summit in six years.
On November 21, 1985, the two sides issued a joint statement that said, "a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought," according to the American Presidency Project's copy of the document.
Geneva produced no big arms treaty that week. Gorbachev said they had "laid the first few bricks."
The two sides signed 41 cultural exchange agreements and agreed to keep meeting.
The next summit was Reykjavik in 1986. Those first bricks led to real cuts later in that decade.
So a sentence with no treaty attached can still matter, if the meetings keep going.
And the leaders gave no date for the next one.
In his meeting with Trump before the three-way talks, Xi warned that the United States must "do the right thing" on Taiwan to avoid major setbacks, Reuters reports.
The trade truce between Washington and Beijing has been extended to January 10, 2027, Al Jazeera reports.
Chinese exports of rare earths to the United States are about 75 percent below normal, according to the think tank CSIS.
Rare earths go into everything from cars to fighter jets. Trump was also expected to press Xi to release two detained Americans, CBS reported before the summit.
No release has been announced. So the nuclear sentence came with a lot of unfinished business.
The last time three leaders this powerful met to settle the world, it did not end the way America hoped.
In February 1945, Franklin Roosevelt, Winston Churchill and Joseph Stalin met at Yalta, in Crimea, from February 4 to February 11.
Germany's defeat was assured. They agreed to split Germany into zones, to found the United Nations, and Stalin agreed to join the war against Japan two or three months after Germany surrendered.
Stalin also promised free elections in Poland. He did not keep that promise, and Poland became a communist state.
In the United States, Yalta was criticized for years, especially by Republicans.
The lesson many Americans drew: a signed promise from Moscow is only as good as what happens after the cameras leave.
That is the test for Shenzhen too, and the clock started that day.
Trump leaves China with a first-ever three-way meeting, a nuclear sentence matching Reagan's, and a pledge on oil supplies.
He does not leave with a nuclear treaty, a Ukraine deal or an Iran deal.
Putin said he has no doubt Trump wants to end the Ukraine war.
Russia and Ukraine held direct talks in Abu Dhabi on November 10 and 11, the first in that format since February, Al Jazeera reports.
Russia's minimum demands, according to one analyst, are full control of the Donbas region and Ukraine dropping its NATO bid.
That gap is still wide. And on Iran, the talks remain stuck.
So the question is whether this summit is a beginning, like Geneva, or a picture, like Yalta.
So here is where it stands. Trump, Xi and Putin signed a four-point declaration: nuclear-risk dialogue, UN reform, more oil, and limits on international courts.
It says nuclear war must never be fought. It says nothing about Ukraine or Iran, and sets no timetable.
No date has been set for the nuclear dialogue. No arms treaty is in place since New START lapsed.
The US-China trade truce runs to January 10, 2027. The Iran talks are stuck on Hormuz.
