Admiral Bobby Ray Inman, who ran the National Security Agency under President Carter and was deputy director of the CIA under President Reagan, has died.
He was 95. His son, Tom Inman, confirmed the death, the Austin American-Statesman reports.
The New York Times called him a well-regarded spy chief who turned down President Bill Clinton's nomination to be secretary of defense.
The way he turned it down was one of the strangest afternoons Washington has ever seen.
In the next minutes: what he said at that press conference, how a boy from a Texas gas station became the first naval intelligence officer to wear four stars, and the bet that helped turn Austin into a tech city.
The Statesman reported the death on November 19, 2026. It did not give a cause or a place of death.
Inman had lived in Austin for decades. Texas Congressman Michael McCaul wrote that he was "very sad to hear of the passing of my friend, Admiral Bobby Ray Inman — one of our nation's most consequential leaders in the intelligence community."
McCaul added: "With his passing, the world lost a giant, and I lost a mentor."
The New York Times, in its obituary summary, called him "a well-regarded director of the National Security Agency and deputy director of central intelligence."
But for most Americans who remember his name, one moment comes first.
In December, President Clinton announced Inman as his choice for secretary of defense, to replace Les Aspin.
At first, he had support from both parties. Then, on January 18, 1994, Inman called a press conference and withdrew.
He did not go quietly. He attacked William Safire, the New York Times columnist who had written a harsh column about him, called "Cold Comfort Level."
Inman suggested Safire had recruited Senator Bob Dole to mount what he called a "vitriolic attack" on him.
He said Dole and Senator Trent Lott planned to "turn up the heat" on his nomination.
All of this was live on television. And the reaction in Washington was immediate.
Senator Dole said: "I have no idea what's gotten into Bobby Inman."
Senator Lott said: "I am floored by Inman's bizarre press conference." An unnamed White House aide was quoted as saying: "Most of us were glued to the tube, our mouths open in shock."
These reactions come from press coverage at the time, as collected in his Wikipedia biography.
Here was a four-star admiral, a man who had kept the nation's biggest secrets for decades, accusing a newspaper columnist and two senators of a plot, on camera.
The White House had to find another defense secretary. But why did a man with his record walk away at all?
The Statesman reports that his business ties to a defense contractor caught up in a major fraud scandal drew scrutiny, and that he was not accused of wrongdoing.
According to his Wikipedia biography, critics pointed to his seat on the board of International Signal and Control, a company involved in illegal exports.
Its chief executive, James Guerin, was later sentenced to 15 years in prison.
Inman himself was never charged. His supporters said he simply refused to be put through a public fight.
His critics said the press conference showed a thin skin. Either way, it ended his Washington career at the very top.
Clinton went on to name William Perry, then the deputy secretary of defense, to the job instead.
For Inman, there would be no more confirmation hearings. To understand how high he had climbed, you have to see where he came from.
Inman spent 31 years in uniform, the Statesman and his biography say, and rose through naval intelligence.
He became director of Naval Intelligence, then vice director of the Defense Intelligence Agency.
President Carter made him director of the National Security Agency, the code-breaking and eavesdropping agency at Fort Meade, Maryland.
He ran it for nearly four years. Then President Reagan made him deputy director of the Central Intelligence Agency, the number-two job at Langley, for about 16 months.
He was the first naval intelligence specialist ever to reach the rank of four-star admiral.
When he retired from the Navy, he did not slow down. But before the bet, there were the fights inside the secret world.
At the NSA, Inman ran an agency most Americans had barely heard of.
Its job was to break foreign codes and listen to foreign communications.
According to his biography, he clashed early with university researchers who were publishing new methods of encryption, the math that now protects bank cards and online shopping.
The agency warned them. Over time, the NSA and the academics found ways to work side by side.
He also granted a security clearance to an openly gay NSA linguist at a time when that was almost unheard of in the intelligence world, his biography notes.
Both decisions said something about the man: he was willing to break with habit when he thought the old rules no longer fit.
Then he went home to Texas and made a bet.
Inman helped bring the Microelectronics and Computer Technology Corporation, known as MCC, to Austin in 1983, the Statesman reports.
It was a computer research consortium, and it brought hundreds of jobs.
It also helped attract more technology investment, the paper reports, including the semiconductor consortium Sematech.
He served as MCC's chairman and chief executive for four years. After that, he ran a company called Westmark Systems for three years, and then spent decades investing in young companies as managing director of Limestone Ventures, according to his biography.
He later chaired the Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas and sat on the boards of Dell and SBC, according to his biography.
He was inducted into the Austin Tech Hall of Fame. When people talk about Austin as a tech city, part of that story starts with a retired spy chief.
And that spy chief started with almost nothing.

Bobby Ray Inman was born on April 4, 1931, in Rhonesboro, Texas, the Statesman reports.
His father owned and ran a gas station, according to his biography.
He graduated from high school in Mineola, Texas, in 1946, at just 15.
He graduated from the University of Texas at 19. Then he joined the Navy, during the Korean War, in 1951.
A small-town East Texas boy, far from any ocean and far from Washington, ended up running the most secret agency in America.
That is the kind of American story the men of his generation recognized.
And he never stopped explaining the world to people who wanted to listen.
In retirement, Inman gave monthly intelligence briefings at Querencia, a retirement community in Austin's Barton Creek area, the Statesman reports.
He would speak for 40 minutes without notes. Rich Oppel, a former editor of the Statesman, said: "You would think that he was reading from the daily CIA briefing of the president, but he wasn't."
Imagine sitting in that room: a man who had read the real president's briefing, explaining the news of the month to his neighbors.
He taught at the University of Texas, too, and twice served as interim dean of the LBJ School of Public Affairs.
And earlier in his career, he was handed one of the hardest assignments a government can give.
In 1983, a truck bomb destroyed the U.S. Marine barracks in Beirut, and the American embassy there had been bombed months earlier.
In the aftermath, the State Department asked Inman to lead a panel on embassy security, according to his biography.
Its findings, published in 1985, became known as the Inman Report, and the fortress-like U.S. embassies built in the years after are often traced to it.
Later, from 1991 to 1993, he served as acting chairman of the President's Foreign Intelligence Advisory Board.
Few Americans knew his face. Many lived in a world shaped by his work.
Inman returned to the University of Texas as a professor, the Statesman reports, and held the LBJ Centennial Chair in National Policy, according to his biography.
He retired from teaching in 2021, at 90. Students who sat in his classes heard Cold War history from a man who had been inside it: the code-breaking, the Soviet threat, the embassy bombings, and the politics of Washington at its roughest.
His medals included the Navy Distinguished Service Medal, the Defense Superior Service Medal and the Legion of Merit, according to his biography.
Even late in life, he spoke out: he publicly criticized warrantless wiretapping, according to his biography, a striking position for a former NSA director.
