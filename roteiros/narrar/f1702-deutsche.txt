More than 15 million internal files from Deutsche Bank, the German bank that lent Donald Trump more than 2 billion dollars over two decades, have leaked to reporters.
The Financial Times reports the files show the bank processed 582 million dollars in payments linked to Jeffrey Epstein and later flagged them as suspicious.
They also show that in 2017 the bank sent one of President Trump's tax documents to another client's accountant, and never told the White House.
Speaking in Manila on November 17, Trump called it "illegal," Reuters reports.
The bank's shares fell more than 6 percent in Frankfurt. In the next minutes: what is in the files, what the bank stands to lose, and how its last Epstein bill and the last Trump tax leak ended.
The leak is called "Inside Deutsche Bank." It covers more than 15 million files: emails, board minutes and internal investigations, the New York Times reports.
Two confidential whistleblowers gave them to two journalists, from India and South Africa.
More than 200 journalists at outlets including the Financial Times, Der Spiegel, Le Monde, The New York Times and Germany's ZDF spent months on them.
Reuters said it could not immediately verify the reports. The bank answered the same day.
"The matters which are in focus of the media reports date back many years, in some cases as much as two decades," it said.
"Deutsche Bank is a different bank today." The first file that hit Washington was about the President.
In March 2017, during Trump's first term, Deutsche Bank sent one of his tax documents to the accountant of another client by mistake, the Financial Times reports.
According to the reports, the bank did not tell the White House.
Trump was asked about it in Manila, where he is attending summits before his trip to China.
He called the disclosure "illegal," according to Reuters. Deutsche Bank declined to comment on his reaction, Euronews reports.
The President's tax papers have been at the center of fights in Washington for a decade.
This time, the leak did not come from the IRS. It came from his own lender.
And the bigger number in the files is about Epstein.
The Financial Times reports Deutsche Bank processed 582 million dollars, about 528 million euros, in payments linked to Jeffrey Epstein, and later flagged them as suspicious.
TokenPost notes that suspicious activity reports flag transactions for review. They do not prove a crime.
Epstein died in jail before trial. None of the reports in the files accuse any other client of a crime.
But 582 million dollars is a number that will bring questions from Congress and from regulators.
And the files show the bank was making another risky bet at the same time.
The records show Deutsche Bank tried to grow its business in Russia shortly before Russia's full invasion of Ukraine, Euronews reports, despite internal warnings about money-laundering and regulatory risks.
The bank's current CEO, Christian Sewing, was part of the working group that prepared that plan.
A month after the invasion, the bank said it would wind down its Russian business.
Its own annual report shows it still had 162 full-time staff in Russia at the end of 2025, and made 61 million euros in pre-tax profit there that year.
The bank says it worked with regulators and followed the rules. That defense has to hold up in front of its own government.
Start with the stock. Deutsche Bank shares fell more than 6 percent in Frankfurt on November 17, then pared losses to about 3.8 percent by early afternoon, Euronews reports.
Before the market opened, Reuters had them down 2.7 percent. The bank says it spent 7 billion euros on stronger internal controls between 2019 and 2025, and expects no significant financial impact from the reports.
Last year it made 9.7 billion euros before taxes, more than double the year before.
Chairman Alexander Wynaendts backed Sewing. Germany's finance minister, Lars Klingbeil, called for full transparency about past control failures, and for regulators and law enforcement to take a close look.
A bank's stock can recover in a week. A regulator's file can stay open for years.
And the President has his own view of what the bank did.
Is it illegal? In the United States, an IRS employee who leaks a tax return can go to prison.
A bank sending a document to the wrong person by mistake is a different legal question, and no charges have been announced.
The President's anger has history. In January of this year, Trump sued the IRS and the Treasury Department as a private citizen, seeking 10 billion dollars in damages over an earlier leak of his returns.
That case is still pending. Trump's supporters say a sitting president's private papers should be protected like anyone else's.
Critics of the bank say the bigger scandal is Epstein's money. Both sides agree on one thing: someone at the bank made a mistake with the President's tax paper.
And Trump's tax paper is not the bank's only problem. Its Epstein history goes back further.
This is not the bank's first Epstein bill. In July 2020, New York's Department of Financial Services fined Deutsche Bank 150 million dollars over its dealings with Jeffrey Epstein, finding it had ignored red flags.
Epstein had been a client from 2013, years after his 2008 guilty plea in Florida.
The bank paid. It promised it had learned its lesson. Six years later, a leak puts a far bigger dollar figure on those same years: 582 million dollars in payments it later called suspicious.
Regulators will want to know whether that number was known when the 2020 fine was set.
