The Treasury Secretary of the United States has given Nvidia's board ten days.
Scott Bessent said on November 15, 2026, that he wants answers from the board and from its outside lawyers within ten days.
He said the next CEO should come from outside the company. And he said that if Nvidia were a bank, "a lot of the executives would be removed."
At the same time, the Financial Times reports that Jensen Huang, the man who built Nvidia and left as CEO this month, has talked with friends about coming back, and that his allies have set up a war room to make it happen.
Nvidia has lost more than 1.8 trillion dollars in market value since its October peak.
Its earnings come out this week. Huang has not been charged with any crime.
But the fight over who runs the world's most valuable chip company has now reached the Treasury.
Scott Bessent spoke on Fox on November 15, 2026. His words were direct.
"I think we need answers in the next 10 days from the board and from the outside counsel."
The outside counsel is Wachtell, Lipton, the New York law firm the board hired to investigate.
Bessent questioned how Nvidia handled compliance and how its chips ended up with shell companies.
He also said, according to Al Jazeera, "We have all the rules we need; we just need them enforced."
Ten days from that interview was November 25, two days before Thanksgiving.
A Treasury Secretary does not usually set a public deadline for a private company's board.
That is what makes this different. It is no longer only Congress asking questions.
The man who runs the nation's money is now asking them out loud, on television, with a date attached.
Then Bessent went further. "This makes me think that, as the board conducts a search for a new leader, that they need to go outside the company."
That one sentence cuts straight against Huang's allies, who want him back, or want one of his own people in the chair.
Bessent compared Nvidia to a bank. Banks are watched by the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, and he said that under those rules, "a lot of the executives would be removed."
He also asked about the company's culture. "I want to keep coming back to what was the culture at Nvidia," he said.
According to Billionaires.Africa, citing the FT, Bessent called for a "dramatic shakeup."
Huang co-founded Nvidia in 1993 and led it for 33 years. Now a cabinet member is telling its board, in public, not to hire from inside.
Bessent also raised money questions. He suggested Nvidia's China sales may have "goosed" its earnings.
Fortune reports he pointed to a surge in sales booked in Singapore while sales to China fell.
The worry is simple. If chips billed to Singapore ended up in China, the numbers on the books would not show where the chips really went.
Nvidia has said earlier claims were "thoroughly investigated and debunked." China's foreign ministry called the claims "entirely groundless, speculative and intended to smear and malign China."
Megaspeed, the Singapore firm named in earlier reports, has denied wrongdoing. None of this has been proven.
But Bessent also hit a fellow cabinet member. On November 13, Fortune reports, he accused Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick of dragging his feet.
CNBC reported that Lutnick's sons at Cantor Fitzgerald arranged financing for Nvidia partners.
Two cabinet members, one company, one fight.
While Washington pushes in one direction, Huang's camp is pushing in the other.
The Financial Times reports that Huang, who is 63, has talked with confidants about returning.
His allies have built what the FT calls a war room. Its goal is to lobby the board and to seek support from the White House.
The FT reports that Huang knows about it and supports it. The FT also reported that the board has approached Arm's CEO, Rene Haas, as a possible outside pick.
Nvidia says it has not formally engaged any candidates. The board is still choosing a search firm.
Wachtell is still investigating. And the President has said nothing yet about Nvidia's next CEO.
When Apple named its new leader, Donald Trump wrote, "A Legend replaces a Legend."
Huang's allies want to hear something like that. What they heard on November 15 came from his own Treasury Secretary, and it went the other way.
Outside the boardroom, the public push keeps growing. The Stand with Jensen petition has passed 170,000 signatures, according to CNBC.
Elon Musk, Epic Games CEO Tim Sweeney and Uber co-founder Travis Kalanick have backed Huang in public.
More than 7,500 of the signers say they work at Nvidia. But a petition does not pick a CEO.
The board does. And the board now has a deadline from the Treasury, a federal investigation over the chips, and congressional committees waiting.
Huang agreed to talk to the House, according to Bloomberg. No date is set.
For a man who once owned every stage he walked onto, the stage is now a room in Washington, and he is not the one holding the microphone.
Washington has done this before. In September 2016, Wells Fargo admitted its workers had opened about two million accounts without customers' consent.
Regulators fined the bank 185 million dollars. On September 20, 2016, CEO John Stumpf sat in front of the Senate Banking Committee, and Senator Elizabeth Warren questioned him.
Nine days later, he went back to Congress and said he would give up at least 41 million dollars in pay.
It was not enough. On October 12, 2016, Stumpf resigned as chairman and CEO.
The pressure did not stop there. In January 2020, the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency fined him 17.5 million dollars, the largest penalty it had ever taken from one person, and he agreed to a lifetime ban from banking.
That is the agency Bessent named on Fox. Stumpf left with more than 130 million dollars.
He never ran a bank again. And Stumpf's fall started with one hearing, which is exactly what Huang now faces.
But first, the money.
So what does Huang lose now? Start with the stock. Nvidia closed at 162 dollars and 85 cents on November 13, 2026.
That is down more than 30 percent from its October 6 peak, and more than 1.8 trillion dollars in value is gone.
Fortune reports the stock is down 24 percent in a month. Huang owns about 3.5 percent of the company, so every drop hits him directly.
Bloomberg reported he had already forfeited about 108 million dollars when he left.
Earnings were set for November 17, 2026. Morgan Stanley says 97 percent of Nvidia's revenue depends on AI.
UBS analyst Tim Arcuri says more than 50 billion dollars is at stake.
And one trader is already winning. The FT reports an 8 million dollar bet against Nvidia, placed on October 12, is now worth about 200 million.
Huang has not been accused of any crime. But his name, his fortune and his company are now tied to a deadline he does not control.

There is another time Washington chose a CEO's fate. On March 29, 2009, Rick Wagoner agreed to step down as chairman and CEO of General Motors.
The White House had asked him to go, as part of a deal for more short-term money.
Wagoner had run GM since June 2000. On his watch the company lost more than 82 billion dollars, and its market value fell more than 90 percent.
One day after he resigned, on March 30, the government rejected GM's plan and gave the company 60 days to fix it or face bankruptcy.
GM filed for bankruptcy within months. Wagoner was replaced from inside by Fritz Henderson, GM's president.
He left with a package worth more than 10 million dollars, including 1.65 million a year in benefits for five years.
The lesson for 2026 is simple. When Washington has leverage, it can decide who sits in the corner office.
GM had taken federal loans. Nvidia has not. Its leverage is different: export licenses, Commerce rules and the chips it needs to sell.
