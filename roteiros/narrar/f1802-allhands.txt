Nvidia's interim CEO, Colette Kress, stood in front of her employees on November 18, 2026, and told them, "let's move forward," according to CNBC.
It was the company's first all-hands meeting since Jensen Huang resigned. One day earlier, the board said it does not intend to bring him back.
But many of the people in that room want him back. An online petition for his return has nearly 200,000 signatures, and more than 10,000 signers say they are current Nvidia employees, CoinDesk reports, citing CNBC.
Nvidia has about 42,000 workers. In the next minutes: what Kress promised the staff, the warning one senior employee sent the board, and how it ended the last time American workers rose up to bring a fired boss back.
The meeting lasted about an hour, CNBC reports. It was held in person and streamed on Microsoft Teams.
Kress recapped the November 17 earnings and called it a "great quarter."
That quarter brought in 115.8 billion dollars in sales. Then she told the staff, "let's move forward."
She also raised the subject of employee pay raises, CNBC reports. A separate report, attributed to Wired, says Nvidia promised raises and more equity at the meeting.
Neither report gives the amounts. Board chair Harvey Jones spoke about the search for the next CEO.
Other longtime leaders spoke too, including co-founder Chris Malachowsky, who started the company with Huang in 1993.
What nobody on stage did, as far as the reports show, was promise Huang's return.
And the room noticed.
One attendee told CNBC that the mood in the room favored Huang's return.
Another said employees welcomed hearing from the leadership team and getting more visibility into who runs the company.
It was not clear beforehand whether employees would even be allowed to ask questions, CoinDesk reported, citing CNBC.
A former employee said Huang used to take employee questions himself at these meetings, held the morning after every earnings report.
This time the man who held them for 33 years was not there.
Some employees had told CNBC before the meeting that they planned to voice their dissatisfaction.
Nvidia declined to comment. Shares fell about 2 percent the next day, CNBC reports, one day after a record quarter.
So what is it that the staff wanted to hear, and did not?
On the November 17 earnings call, Jones put it plainly: "The Nvidia board unanimously accepted his resignation and does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO."
That is the quote, as reported by CNBC. The word "unanimously" matters.
It means no director voted to keep him. On the same day, Huang said for the first time that he would be "honored to answer these calls and return as CEO."
So the founder says yes, and the board says no. Huang still owns about 870 million shares, roughly 3.5 percent of the company, Fortune reported.
His shares carry no special voting power, Forbes reported. He cannot vote his way back.
He needs the board to change its mind, and the staff are trying to make that happen.
The online petition to bring Huang back has gathered nearly 200,000 signatures since the weekend before last, CoinDesk reports, citing CNBC.
More than 10,000 of the signers say they are current Nvidia employees.
Nvidia has about 42,000 employees. If those self-reported numbers hold, that is roughly one worker in four.
Petitions can be signed by anyone, and the employee claims cannot be checked from the outside.
But several employees have also posted on X backing Huang, using their own names.
One robotics engineer wrote that Huang's departure led him to decide to leave too.
A former employee of more than 15 years described Nvidia's engineers as extremely loyal to Huang.
That loyalty is the board's biggest problem, and one senior employee put it in writing.
A senior Nvidia employee wrote to the board and to top executives, according to The Wall Street Journal, as cited by CoinDesk.
The message warned that if the board does not reinstate Huang, the company could lose "irreplaceable talent."
At a chip company, the talent is the product. Nvidia's newest systems, like Vera Rubin, added nearly 20 billion dollars in sales in a single quarter, according to Kress.
The engineers who designed them can walk across the street to a rival.
That is why the pay raises matter. Raises and more equity are a classic way to keep people from leaving during a leadership fight.
Kress is a former finance chief. She knows exactly what the stock awards are worth.
But the staff are not the only ones pushing for Huang.
Elon Musk has called for Huang's reinstatement, CoinDesk reports. So have Garry Tan, who runs the startup investor Y Combinator, and Tim Sweeney, the head of Epic Games, along with at least two major investors.
President Trump said he would "not mind" Huang returning. On the other side, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said on November 15 that the next CEO should come from outside the company, and gave the board ten days to answer his questions.
The board has hired lawyers to investigate. China calls the spy claims "entirely groundless."
Megaspeed, the Singapore firm named in earlier reports, denies wrongdoing. Huang has not been charged with any crime.
So the fight is between the board on one side and the workers, the investors and some of the most famous men in tech on the other.

Workers have done this before. On June 23, 2014, the board of Market Basket, the New England grocery chain, fired its CEO, Arthur T.
Demoulas. The next day, about 300 employees rallied outside the flagship store in Chelsea, Massachusetts, and six top managers quit.
On July 18, warehouse workers and drivers walked off the job, and shelves went bare.
Protests grew to as many as 5,000 employees and customers. The company fired seven organizers.
It did not stop them. On August 27, 2014, the other shareholders agreed to sell their 50.5 percent stake to Demoulas and his sisters for 1.5 billion dollars.
He got his company back. But he had to buy it. Huang has the shares to try, and that is where the comparison gets interesting.
Demoulas won because his family already controlled almost half of Market Basket.
Huang owns about 3.5 percent of Nvidia. At around 160 dollars a share, that is roughly 139 billion dollars on paper.
It is the largest stake of any individual, Fortune has reported, but it is nowhere near control.
Nvidia is worth trillions. No one person can buy it. So the Market Basket path is closed to him.
What is left is pressure: workers, investors, and Washington. The board answered that pressure on November 17 with one word, "unanimously."
But boards have changed their minds before, and one famous coffee company did exactly that.
Howard Schultz built Starbucks into a national chain, then stepped down as CEO on June 1, 2000.
The company kept growing, but by the mid-2000s it was losing ground in the coffee wars with McDonald's and Dunkin'.
Its stock fell about 75 percent from 2006 to 2008. On January 7, 2008, the board brought Schultz back as CEO.
He fired executives, closed hundreds of stores, and even temporarily closed every U.S. store to retrain baristas in making espresso.
From 2008 to 2017, Starbucks added nearly 100 billion dollars in market value under him.
The lesson: boards bring founders back when the company is failing. Nvidia, on November 17, reported the best quarter in its history.
That makes Huang's road harder, and the next few dates will show how hard.
November 25 is the end of Bessent's ten-day window for the board's answers.
Huang was expected to testify behind closed doors in early December 2026, Democracy Now reported, about his relationship with a woman alleged to have ties to Chinese intelligence.
Huang said he made serious personal mistakes and would cooperate with the board's investigation.
China calls the spy claims "entirely groundless." December 2 is the record date for Nvidia's next dividend, 25 cents a share.
And Nvidia said it would file its full quarterly report with regulators "in the coming weeks."
Each of those dates is a chance for the pressure to rise or fade.
The workers who signed the petition will be watching every one. So will the 10,000-plus who say they work there.
So here is where it stands. Kress told Nvidia's staff to move forward and talked about raises.
The board says, unanimously, that Huang is not coming back. Nearly 200,000 people, more than 10,000 of them saying they are Nvidia employees, say he should.
The board still has to choose a permanent CEO, and Bessent wants an outsider.
The investigation by the board's lawyers continues. The petition keeps growing. If engineers start to leave, the board will feel it in the next product cycle.
If they stay, the board's bet on moving forward will look right.
Kress has one big advantage: the numbers. Nvidia forecast 130 billion dollars in sales for next quarter, more than Wall Street expected, and it has about 227 billion dollars left to buy back its own stock.
Those buybacks and the raises help every employee who holds Nvidia shares.
Money buys time. It does not buy loyalty. At the time of this report, Huang was not charged with anything, still owned his shares, and said he would be honored to return.
The workers want him. The board does not.
Where it stands: the board says no, and thousands of Nvidia workers say yes.
