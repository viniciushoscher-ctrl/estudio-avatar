The U.S. Navy may never go back to the base it has used to run the Persian Gulf for generations.
On November 27, 2026, The Washington Post reported that the Pentagon was reassessing the future of Naval Support Activity Bahrain, home of the Fifth Fleet, after Iranian missiles and drones caused damage estimated at 400 million dollars or more.
More than 8,000 Americans once lived and worked there. At the time of the report, only a fraction remained.
In the next minutes: what the strikes actually destroyed, where the fleet operated from after the damage, and the option on the table that would end a presence older than most of the people watching.
According to the Post's report, as summarized by Newsmax and by Kuwait's Arab Times, two Navy officials said the service had only recently begun assessing its future presence in Bahrain, and had made no specific long-term decisions.
One of them said the headquarters' job could be performed from the United States, without fully rebuilding the installation, while ties to the Gulf are kept alive through periodic visits.
Asked for comment, the Navy said it had "nothing to announce at this time" and could not speak to internal deliberations.
At the time of the report, the war with Iran was nine months old.
It began on February 28, 2026, when American and Israeli forces struck Iran and Tehran answered by firing at Bahrain and other Gulf states.
And one official explained, in a single sentence, why nobody was in a hurry to return.
Newsmax reports that nine months into the war, Iran had resumed strikes on Bahrain in the weeks before the report.
That is the backdrop for the quote that leads every account of this story.
"Until all of this is resolved, we're not going anywhere near there," one Navy official told the Post.
Arab Times carries the same line. The official added: "It just doesn't make any sense."
The numbers explain the caution. Of the more than 8,000 military personnel, family members and civilian contractors once based at the installation, only a small fraction were still there, according to the Post.
Nearby neighborhoods are described as largely deserted. According to the Post, 2 civilians had been killed and dozens wounded in Iranian attacks on Bahrain, and 2 Defense Department employees were among the wounded.
So what exactly did the missiles hit?
The first public estimate did not come from the Pentagon. It came from reporters.
In June 2026, the Wall Street Journal analyzed satellite images and social media footage and concluded that the base had been targeted numerous times between late February and June, according to National Security Journal.
The command headquarters and at least a dozen other buildings were heavily damaged, along with two satellite communications terminals.
The think tank CSIS put the value of each of those terminals at about 20 million dollars.
The Journal's total: more than 400 million dollars. Navy Times, citing the same investigation, described it as roughly 400 million dollars in construction costs to repair barracks, warehouses, communication towers and the headquarters itself.
The Congressional Research Service cited preliminary estimates of 400 million dollars or more.
And Bahrain was only one base.
National Security Journal reports that CSIS estimated between 2.2 billion and 5.1 billion dollars in damage to American bases across the region, inside a total war cost of about 40 billion dollars.
A spokesman for Central Command, Navy Captain Tim Hawkins, put the scale of the attacks this way: Iran, he said, "shot more than 8,000 missiles and drones."
Early on, according to Navy Times, the Pentagon said there were no fatalities from the strike on Bahrain and that operations were not significantly affected.
Months later, the people in charge of the Navy started saying something very different, and they said it out loud.
At a town hall reported by Navy Times on September 1, 2026, the Chief of Naval Operations, Admiral Daryl Caudle, was asked about Bahrain.
"We're not getting back in there anytime soon," he said. "I just want to be completely honest with that."
That same month, acting Navy Secretary Hung Cao told The Epoch Times that Iran "blew the hell out of Bahrain," according to Newsmax.
And the commander of the Fifth Fleet itself, Vice Admiral Curt Renshaw, admitted the service was "making it up as we go," Navy Times reported, because nothing had been planned for a disruption this long.
Which raises the obvious question. If the fleet was not in Bahrain, where was it?
According to Navy Times, after the damage the Navy began relying on Diego Garcia, the island base in the Indian Ocean, more than 2,000 miles from the carrier strike groups it supports.
That is a very long supply line for a fleet at war.
At an August 11, 2026 town hall in San Diego with acting Secretary Cao, families of sailors on the carrier USS Abraham Lincoln raised concerns about food and morale aboard ship, Navy Times reported.
The paper also reported that thousands of troops and family members were still in temporary lodging.
For the families, the war did not end at the gate. It followed them home.
On March 2, 2026, two days after the first strikes, the State Department ordered non-emergency American government personnel and their families to leave Bahrain, according to Newsmax.
Some left behind their possessions. Some left behind their pets. According to the Post, the neighborhoods they lived in sat largely empty.
For a lot of Navy families, Bahrain was not a combat zone.
It was a two- or three-year tour with a food court and a Navy Exchange.
That ordinary life was the thing that disappeared. So what were the choices?

The reporting points to three paths, and none had been chosen. One: rebuild.
National Security Journal says planners have weighed rebuilding some structures underground and not rebuilding others at all.
Two: shrink. The Post reported back in August 2026 that the Pentagon had weighed reducing the American footprint in the Gulf, including shifting forces to Israel and Jordan.
Three: leave the headquarters at home. That is the option the Navy official described, with the command run from the United States and the Gulf visited by ships from time to time.
Each path has a price, and the last one has a price nobody had put a number on.
To understand why, you have to know how long America has been there.
The site was a British naval base, HMS Jufair, opened in 1935, according to the base's history on Wikipedia.
American ships were there during World War Two, and from 1948 the posting was known as the U.S.
Middle East Force. Newsmax, citing the Congressional Research Service, says Bahrain has hosted an American naval command headquarters since 1948.
When Britain pulled out in 1971, the U.S. Navy took over the whole site, about 10 acres.
For the men watching this who served in the Gulf, that base was where the Navy's Middle East story began.
And for the nine months before this report, it was a target.
The first strike came on February 28, 2026, the first day of the war, with missiles and one-way attack drones, Navy Times reports.
It did not stop. According to a timeline of the war compiled on Wikipedia from news reports, Iran said it targeted the Fifth Fleet headquarters again on June 3; Central Command said it shot down the incoming missiles and drones.
On June 6, Central Command said it shot down six of seven ballistic missiles aimed at a Navy site.
By November 2026, according to Newsmax, strikes on Bahrain had resumed in recent weeks.
A retired vice admiral, John Miller, told National Security Journal: "I think there are some things we would do differently."
The base was built for a different kind of war.
According to Newsmax, the Bahrain headquarters supported operations in the Gulf War of 1991 and the Iraq War that began in 2003, and later the effort to deter Iran and its proxies.
It kept growing. According to the base history, a 580 million dollar expansion launched in 2010 was meant to roughly double a 62-acre facility.
That is the investment sitting damaged behind the fences. And just outside those fences, there was a whole town that was built around the Americans.
The Post spoke with Ali Asgar, a tailor who began making suits for Americans in 2000.
"They were our family," he said, according to Newsmax. "We miss them, and we want them to come back."
Local merchants told the Post that tailors, gold dealers and Persian carpet sellers had all taken heavy losses.
Bahrain's government declined to discuss the base's future, but a spokesperson said the two countries "share a strategic partnership spanning eight decades," a relationship it described as still strong.
Eighty years of partnership, and a nine-month war. That was the balance the Pentagon was weighing.
So here is what was known at the time of the report.
The Post reports the Navy was reassessing the base. The damage is estimated at 400 million dollars or more.
Of more than 8,000 Americans once there, only a fraction remained. The Navy's top admiral said they were not going back anytime soon.
And one option on the table was to run the fleet from the United States.
Where it stood on November 27, 2026: the base was damaged, the families were gone, and the Navy said it had nothing to announce.
Officials told the Post the assessment had only just begun and no date had been set for a decision; the strikes on Bahrain had resumed in recent weeks.
