Gebhard "Geb" Galle, one of the last survivors of the attack on Pearl Harbor, has died at 105.
He died on November 14, 2026, at his home in Moses Lake, Washington, according to Pacific Historic Parks, the AP and Hawaii News Now report.
On that Sunday morning he was a 20-year-old sailor below deck on the battleship USS Nevada.
On the very same day he died, another survivor, Freeman Johnson, died at 106.
In the next minutes: what Galle saw through a porthole that morning, the order he disobeyed a year later that saved four men, and how many Pearl Harbor survivors are still alive.
Pacific Historic Parks announced Galle's death on Facebook, Stars and Stripes reports.
Freeman Johnson served on the cruiser USS St. Louis and lived in Centerville, Massachusetts, according to the AP.
He was 106. Pacific Historic Parks is the nonprofit partner of the Pearl Harbor National Memorial, the group that keeps track of the survivors and helps bring them back to Hawaii for the anniversaries.
Stars and Stripes called Galle "the second of the few remaining Pearl Harbor survivors to die in the past week."
Two men who stood on ships in that harbor, gone within the same 24 hours, more than eight decades later.
For families of the men who served, every one of these announcements closes a door that will not open again.
And the number of doors left is very small.
An estimated 87,000 military personnel were on Oahu on the morning of December 7, 1941, the AP reports.
Only about a dozen were believed to be alive. According to the AP, the list of known survivors had shrunk to about a dozen names after Ira "Ike" Schab died in December 2025 at 105.
Stars and Stripes says "roughly a dozen veterans of the attack are believed to be living."
Two more were gone. The Spokesman-Review reported in March that Galle was the last living crew member of the USS Nevada, according to Stars and Stripes.
So with his death, the Nevada's crew passes entirely into history. And what that crew did that morning is one of the great stories of the attack.
Galle was a machinist's mate, 20 years old. That Sunday, he was below deck, waiting to go ashore for church, the AP reports.
Through a porthole, he saw planes. His battle station was two decks below, in the engineering spaces.
"Of course, we felt a couple jolts, and that's when we got hit with the torpedo, and we had a few bomb hits," he told the Columbia Basin Herald in 2020, as quoted by the AP, Hawaii News Now and Stars and Stripes.
A machinist's mate keeps the engines, pumps and machinery running, which meant that when the alarm sounded, Galle's place was not on deck with the guns but deep inside the hull.
He could not see the battle. He could only feel it, deep inside a ship that was being torn open around him.
What the Nevada did next made it famous.
The Nevada was "the only battleship that got underway during the attack," Stars and Stripes reports.
While other battleships sat trapped along Battleship Row, the Nevada's crew got her moving toward the harbor entrance.
Hit by a torpedo and then by bombs, she was run aground near the entrance "to prevent it from sinking," Hawaii News Now reports.
According to the Navy, 76 sailors and Marines on the Nevada lost their lives, the AP reports.
Galle came up on deck only after it was over. "After we were beached and everything, and we got up to topside, then we saw all the mess that took place," he said.
He called it "a pretty sad lookout." Within days, the Navy sent him to another ship, and to the hardest moment of his war.
Galle was reassigned "within days" to the cruiser USS Northampton, Stars and Stripes reports.
On November 30, the next year, at the Battle of Tassafaronga near Guadalcanal, two Japanese torpedoes hit the Northampton.
Stars and Stripes reports the Northampton was part of an American cruiser-destroyer force sent out that November night in 1942.
In the dark, the ship was hit and began to flood. Watertight hatches are closed in battle so that water in one compartment cannot sink the whole ship, even if men are still inside.
Galle heard pounding behind a locked hatch. An officer ordered him not to open it.
He opened it anyway. "Come on, get out of there. That equipment can run without you," he told the men inside, according to Stars and Stripes.
The AP and Hawaii News Now report that four sailors escaped. Years later, he said plainly: "I disobeyed orders.
I could've been shot." Four men lived because a young machinist's mate decided their lives mattered more than an order.
But the Northampton was going down.
The Northampton sank in about three hours, Stars and Stripes reports. Galle and about 40 other sailors spent more than a day in two life rafts before they were rescued, Hawaii News Now and Stars and Stripes report.
That is about 20 men to a raft, in open water, after a night battle, waiting and hoping a friendly ship found them first.
He was 21. He went on to serve on the light cruiser USS Mobile through the end of the war.
In all, he fought in 19 of 20 Pacific naval engagements, according to a Navy letter cited by the AP; Hawaii News Now describes it as 19 major engagements across the Pacific.
That is almost the entire war at sea, from the first morning to the last.
The Navy wrote to him about that record only in 2025. And his life began far from any ocean.

Gebhard Galle was born in Germany, the AP reports. In 1928, at age 7, he came to North Dakota.
In 1939, at 18, he joined the U.S. Navy. Two years later, the country he had chosen was attacked, and the boy from the northern plains was in the engine room of a battleship.
Men like Galle, immigrants who served the country that took them in, filled the crews of that generation's Navy.
When the war ended, he went back to the middle of the country and built a life most of his neighbors probably knew nothing about.
He served from before Pearl Harbor until after the surrender, and he was discharged in 1946, Stars and Stripes reports.
But the Navy did not forget.
For his 104th birthday in 2025, Admiral James Kilby, then the acting Chief of Naval Operations, wrote to him.
"Your humility, quick thinking and selfless actions remain a shining example of Navy valor," Kilby wrote, according to the AP.
The letter is where the record of 19 of 20 Pacific engagements comes from.
According to the AP's account of that letter, Galle also witnessed the launch of the Doolittle Raid, the first American air strike on Japan.
A machinist's mate, below decks for most of it, and still the Navy's top officer called him an example.
He received that letter at 104, and he lived to see one more birthday.
Before that, he went back to Hawaii once more to see the harbor where it started.
In December 2016, Galle returned to Pearl Harbor for the 75th anniversary service, the AP and Hawaii News Now report.
Seventy-five years after the attack that killed his shipmates, he had a message about the enemy.
"You can carry your hatred all of your life," he said, according to the AP.
He chose not to. For the men of his generation, that was its own kind of victory: to fight the war, win it, and then come home without carrying it forever.
Back home, he went to work.
After the war, Galle married and opened a refrigeration and air-conditioning business in North Dakota, the AP reports.
Later, he worked as a civil engineer for the Air Force and the U.S.
Army Corps of Engineers, before retiring. He settled in Moses Lake, Washington, where he died.
Like so many of the men who came home, he did not lead with his war story.
Local reporters had to find him. The Columbia Basin Herald interview is the reason we have his words.
Without it, the porthole, the jolts, the beached battleship and the hatch would have gone with him.
That is why historians and veterans groups have spent years recording these men while there was still time.
For Galle, there was time, and he used it to tell the truth plainly: what he felt, what he saw, and the order he broke.
So here is where it stands. Geb Galle, USS Nevada, Pearl Harbor survivor, died at 105 on November 14.
Freeman Johnson died the same day at 106. Roughly a dozen survivors are believed to be alive.
No public funeral or memorial plans have been announced in the reports we found.
On December 7, the nation marks the 85th anniversary of the attack, with fewer survivors than ever.
Each year, the ceremony at Pearl Harbor has had fewer men in the front row.
At the December 7, 2026 ceremony, the Nevada would have no living crew member there at all.
