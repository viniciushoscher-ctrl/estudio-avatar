Revie Sorey, the Chicago Bears guard who blocked for Walter Payton for nine seasons, has died at 73.
According to the Chicago Sun-Times and Fox News, he died on Thanksgiving Day, November 26, 2026, at Northwestern Memorial Hospital in Chicago.
He wore number 69 for nine seasons and played 109 games, every one of them for the Bears.
In the next minutes: what his closest friend said about how he died, the record-breaking afternoon when Payton thanked his linemen by name, and the second career that hundreds of Chicago kids wrote in about after his death.
First, the name, because a lot of fans never heard it said right: according to the Sun-Times, Sorey rhymes with "Stevie Story."
The news came from Anita Efron, whom the Sun-Times describes as his closest friend and "chosen family."
Her husband, Morton Efron, is the Chicago attorney who represented him, the paper says.
According to the Sun-Times, Efron and her family released a statement on November 27 announcing his death.
She did not describe a long illness. "His heart just stopped," she told the paper.
"It was his time." Fox News, citing the Sun-Times, reports the same: he died on Thanksgiving Day at 73 at Northwestern Memorial.
No cause beyond those words had been made public, and no funeral or memorial date had been announced.
What Efron did describe was what happened next on her phone. And that tells you who Revie Sorey was.
"I am getting literally hundreds of texts and emails from people who Revie helped," Efron told the Sun-Times.
Hundreds of messages, and not about blocks or touchdowns. They came from people he helped, she said.
The Sun-Times put that side of him right in its headline: a Bears guard "who blocked for Payton and dedicated his life to helping at-risk kids."
Efron also said everybody talks about his great infectious smile. By the evening of November 27, the Sun-Times had asked readers what made him "more than just an offensive lineman," and published their answers at 7:09 that evening, about seven hours after its obituary went up at 12:05 p.m.
One reader called him an "awesome human." So what did he actually do for those kids?
According to the Sun-Times, Sorey and Efron co-founded the Revie Sorey Trunk Scholarship Program.
The idea was simple. First-generation college students from Chicago, the first in their families to go, often show up on campus with nothing.
So the program sent them off with a trunk. Inside, according to the paper: everything "from computers to bedding."
Fox News describes the same program for first-generation college-bound students. The Sun-Times says hundreds of students received those trunks.
For a kid who has never seen a dorm room, a computer and a set of sheets can be the difference between staying and going home in the first month.
Sorey knew that road himself: he left Brooklyn for a college campus in another state.
And the trunks were only one of his projects.
The Sun-Times reports that Sorey and Efron ran the Revie Sorey All-Pro Football Camp for 27 years.
He also put his name on the Revie Sorey Racquetball Classic, which raised money for local charities.
And for his day job, according to both the Sun-Times and Fox News, he was director of educational services at Ada S.
McKinley Community Services, a youth services organization at 1359 West Washington Boulevard in Chicago.
A man who spent nine years opening holes for the greatest running back in the league spent decades more opening doors.
Then, one day in March, everything stopped.
In March 2012, according to the Sun-Times and Fox News, Sorey suffered a stroke.
The Sun-Times reports it left him unable to speak or walk, needing care around the clock, and that he went through rehabilitation on Chicago's North Side.
He regained some speech, the paper says, though only a limited amount.
His teammates did not forget him. Former Bears Tom Hicks, Brian Baschnagel and Bruce Herron stopped by in 2016 to give him a haircut and some company, according to the Sun-Times.
Hicks told the paper a story that tells you about the man before the stroke: Sorey, he said, "nicknamed himself Dr.
Rock Hollywood." That nickname fit a man who loved a stage. And his biggest stage was a muddy field in November.
The Sun-Times noted one more detail about that Thanksgiving. The day Revie Sorey died, his Bears played, and won.
Chicago beat the Detroit Lions 39 to 35, according to the Sun-Times.
For a man who gave nine seasons to that franchise, it is the kind of ending a teammate might have scripted.
But the tributes were only beginning.
The messages reaching Efron after his death came from people Revie helped, she told the Sun-Times.
Readers sent the paper their own memories, of the player on the field and of the man off it.
One reader, Jerry LaBuy, wrote: "Rest easy, sir." It is a short message, and it is what a lot of Chicago was saying that weekend about number 69.

Sorey was born in Brooklyn and raised in Bedford-Stuyvesant, according to the Sun-Times.
He went to the University of Illinois, where the paper says he was a two-time All-Big Ten selection and an offensive captain.
Wikipedia lists him as a second-team All-American in 1974. In 1975, the Bears drafted him in the fifth round, according to Fox News.
That same year, the Pro Football Hall of Fame says, Chicago used the fourth overall pick on a running back from Jackson State named Walter Payton.
The two rookies arrived together. What happened between them over the next nine years is the reason you know his name.
The Sun-Times kept a column by John Schulian about Payton's early seasons, and in it, Sorey explained what the line was trying to do.
Before one season finale, he said, according to the column, "We concentrated on proving to Walter that we were trying for him so much that we forgot about the game."
Think about that. A whole offensive line playing for one man. They wanted him to know they would do anything to spring him.
One afternoon, they proved it.
On November 20, 1977, at Soldier Field, Walter Payton ran for 275 yards against the Minnesota Vikings, according to the Pro Football Hall of Fame.
The Sun-Times says it broke O.J. Simpson's single-game record of 273, and the Bears won 10 to 7.
Afterward, in Schulian's words, Payton talked about the way his linemen, Jackson and Sorey among them, had mowed down the Vikings for him.
That season, the Hall of Fame says, Payton ran for 1,852 yards and was named the league's Most Valuable Player.
But a record is a single day. The job Sorey did was every Sunday.
Schulian's column, as reprinted by the Sun-Times, gives the details of that afternoon.
Left guard Noah Jackson was in his third season of blocking for Payton.
The longest run of the day, 58 yards, went through right tackle.
And the record itself fell on a four-yard gain, Payton's final carry of the drive.
Four yards. A guard's yards. The kind of gain that only happens when the men in front win their fight at the line.
That was Revie Sorey's job, and it is why his name belongs next to Payton's.
To see the whole picture, look at what that line built over nine years.
Sorey played for the Bears from 1975 to 1983, nine seasons, according to the Sun-Times and Fox News.
Fox reports 109 games and 77 starts, all at guard. The Sun-Times says he earned All-NFC honors.
Over that span, Payton led the league in carries from 1976 to 1979, according to Bleacher Report, and when he retired after 1987 he held the NFL career rushing record, 16,726 yards, the Hall of Fame says.
He made nine Pro Bowls and was a seven-time All-Pro, and he entered the Hall of Fame in 1993.
Every one of those honors was earned behind a line, and for nine of those seasons, number 69 was in it.
Payton died on November 1, 1999. Sorey retired two seasons before the 1985 Bears won the Super Bowl.
Which leaves one question for his fans.
As of November 27, 2026, no funeral or memorial plans had been announced, according to the Sun-Times and Fox News.
The trunk program and the camp were built with Efron, and she is the person speaking for him.
The messages, she says, number in the hundreds. Three of the men he played beside, Hicks, Baschnagel and Herron, went to see him after his stroke, the paper reports.
Their stories, and the fans who saw him play, are how number 69 will be remembered.
