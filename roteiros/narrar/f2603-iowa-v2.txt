The Army destroyed the country's entire stockpile of an explosive so unstable that moving it was considered too dangerous.
At about 9 a.m. on November 25, 2026, soldiers detonated 68 barrels of lead azide, about 5 tons, at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant near Middletown, Iowa, a town of about 350 people.
Families were urged to leave. Three states watched the wind. In the next minutes: how this material sat in Iowa for 17 years until it became a bomb nobody could touch, what the monitors found after the blast, and who pays if the soil tests come back bad.
According to CBS News and ABC News, the detonation happened at about 9 a.m. local time on November 25.
ABC reports the 68 barrels were set off in two simultaneous detonations.
KCRG and CBS put the total at about 5 tons; ABC put it at nearly 9,700 pounds.
KCRG, working with the Associated Press, described it as the nation's entire stockpile of lead azide.
Maj. Gen. Eric Shirley, who commanded the operation, said, according to Fox News, that "all the lead azide barrels were definitively removed."
He reported no secondary explosions, no notable dust kick-outs and no damage to buildings.
But the explosion was never what frightened the people living nearby. The dust was.
Lead azide contains lead, and lead is a neurotoxin. KCRG reminded viewers that lead is "not safe at any level," especially for children and pregnant women.
So the government ringed the area with sensors. According to CBS, the EPA reported zero airborne lead on four monitors about 15 minutes after the blast, and the same readings two hours later.
The Army said FEMA gave an "all clear" around the plant, and that defense agency maps showed "ZERO airborne lead detection."
Fox reports FEMA announced the all clear at 6:31 p.m. Eastern. Missouri Governor Mike Kehoe called the explosion "well-contained."
Iowa Governor Kim Reynolds called it "successful." But air is only half of the test, and the other half takes weeks.
How does an American town end up next to a pile of explosives too dangerous to move?
According to KCRG, citing ATF director Robert Cekada, the lead azide was brought to the Iowa plant in 2009 from bases around the country.
It sat there. CBS, citing the EPA, says the explosives degraded over about 20 years in storage and began to recrystallize.
KCRG reports that a March inspection found the material had partly crystallized, making it sensitive to friction and cold, with a risk of exploding on its own.
ABC says the Army judged that packing and moving the barrels risked an accidental detonation.
KCRG says burning it or neutralizing it in the drums was judged less safe than a controlled blast.
So the only safe option was to blow it up where it sat.
And that is when the fight began.
The original date was November 20, according to ABC and KCRG. KCRG says the date changed three times amid public backlash, and for safety and favorable wind, according to court documents.
The Iowa Environmental Council and the Sierra Club's Iowa chapter filed an emergency motion, ABC reports, arguing the public had not been given proper notice.
A federal appeals court, the 8th Circuit, briefly paused the blast, then lifted that pause on November 23.
The court wrote that "the degraded explosives present a risk of an uncontrolled and unplanned explosion."
CBS reports that falling temperatures were making the material more volatile, according to Acting Army Secretary Adam Telle, who called the November 25 forecast "ideal to protect public health and safety across the region."
So the Army had a narrow window. And the families in its path had to decide whether to stay.
According to KCRG, officials encouraged residents within 5 miles to evacuate. ABC reports that federal officials urged pregnant women, children under six and people with chronic illness to leave, and that up to 100 Iowa National Guard soldiers went door to door to about 150 households.
Fox reports that FEMA told anyone within 20 miles who had not left to shelter in place, an order lifted the evening of November 25.
Iowa declared a disaster in three counties: Des Moines, Lee and Van Buren, KCRG says.
ABC spoke to a Middletown resident who planned to leave with his pregnant wife.
"It's terrifying," he said. Others taped their windows and garage doors. Some pulled their cars over upwind to watch.
The Army's own guidance, ABC reports, said lead could settle across 186 square miles outside the plant.
That is the number the soil tests now have to answer.
To keep the dust down, the Army built a shield around the barrels.
According to CBS, citing the Army, crews used blocks of foam, gel blocks, and two walls of four 16,000-gallon water tanks.
KCRG adds two-story concrete and sand barriers and more than 100,000 gallons of water in all.
The idea was simple: let the blast hit water and foam first, so the lead falls nearby instead of riding the wind.
General Shirley said he wanted the risk "as close to zero as is absolutely humanly possible," CBS and ABC report.
And he gave the reason the Army would not wait any longer: "Inaction is no longer an option."
ATF director Cekada said the operation went "100% as planned." But not everyone was satisfied with how Iowa got here.

Iowa Governor-elect Rob Sand, who had not yet taken office, said, according to ABC, that "Everyday Iowans are entitled to know how Middletown came to this situation."
Fox reports he called the lead-up chaotic and unclear and said Iowans were understandably alarmed.
CBS reports that President Trump said the Army would not proceed without the court's ruling and Sand's approval, although Sand was not required to weigh in.
After the blast, CBS reports, Trump posted that "early reports from Iowa are that the Army's mitigation efforts have far exceeded expectations."
Fox reports Vice President JD Vance blamed the 2009 decision to ship the material to Iowa under the Obama administration, saying the can had been kicked down the road.
The environmental groups, ABC says, chose not to block the blast again but are still pressing for transparency.
And this plant has a longer history than most Iowans know.
According to Wikipedia, the plant was established in November 1940 as the Iowa Ordnance Plant, and production began in 1941.
Production stopped in 1945, at the end of World War II. It started again in 1949 and rose sharply in 1950 for the Korean War.
The Army took over responsibility in 1975. It covers 19,011 acres, with 767 buildings, 271 storage igloos, 143 miles of road and 102 miles of railroad.
Many of you have fathers or uncles who fired shells that came out of plants like this one.
And this is not the first time the plant has made headlines.
The Army says a team of more than 150 agency officials will clean the area and test for contamination, KCRG reports.
Residents can volunteer to have their soil tested, and property owners can file claims for damage.
Fox reports federal agencies will collect air and soil samples over days and weeks, and the Agriculture Department will visit nearby farms.
ABC reports the CDC activated an emergency response to support Iowa's health department.
EPA associate administrator Tom Croci said, according to ABC, that the agency will keep monitoring "for as long as it takes."
No outlet we read reported what the operation cost. That bill, and any claims, will be the next fight.
According to Wikipedia, in August 1989 the plant was placed on the EPA's National Priorities List, the list of the country's most serious contamination sites, after explosives contaminated surface water beyond its boundary.
In March 2020, a fire-training area was flagged for so-called forever chemicals.
And on January 22, 2025, an unplanned explosion hit the plant, with no injuries.
So for families in Des Moines County, the November 2026 blast was not the first warning.
It was the loudest. And the plant's future is about to get bigger, not smaller.
Wikipedia reports that in 2025, a company called MSM North America won a contract to build a 155-millimeter artillery shell plant at the site.
The target is 36,000 shells by September 2029. Those are the same caliber of shells the United States has been sending abroad in large numbers.
So Middletown will keep living next to one of the country's ammunition factories for years to come.
The plant has 25 Army civilians and one soldier on staff, Wikipedia says, with the work done by a contractor, American Ordnance, a Day and Zimmermann subsidiary.
So: about 5 tons of unstable lead azide, stored in Iowa since 2009, were destroyed in a controlled blast on the morning of November 25, 2026.
Monitors found no airborne lead, and FEMA gave the all clear. Soil tests, farm visits and damage claims come next.
