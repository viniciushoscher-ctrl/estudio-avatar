Social Security had started mailing letters telling some Medicare beneficiaries their coverage may end on February 1, 2027.
About 300,000 people are expected to get a preliminary notice, according to the federal rule described by Insurance Business and the Center for Medicare Advocacy.
The cause is a provision in the big tax and spending law signed on July 4, 2025.
In the next minutes: exactly who loses Medicare and who keeps it, whether your Social Security check is touched, and what to do if one of these letters lands in your mailbox by mistake.
The Washington Post reported on the letters on November 24, and Insurance Business and IBTimes UK quoted the wording.
One letter reads: "A review of our records shows you may no longer qualify for Medicare due to a change in the law."
IBTimes stresses that these notices are preliminary, and "do not mean coverage has already been terminated."
According to Social Security guidance quoted by the Center for Medicare Advocacy, coverage "may end February 1, 2027, unless they establish eligibility" within the time given.
Insurance Business says the mailings began in mid-November. So this is a warning, not a cancellation.
But the people receiving it want to know one thing first: why me?
The law narrowed who can get Medicare based on immigration status. According to the Center for Medicare Advocacy, citing Social Security, these groups keep Medicare: U.S. citizens and nationals, lawful permanent residents, meaning green card holders, Cuban-Haitian entrants, and migrants from the Compact of Free Association islands.
The groups that lose eligibility on that basis alone, according to all three reports, are refugees, people granted asylum, holders of Temporary Protected Status, and certain survivors of human trafficking.
Insurance Business says the letters go to people who earned Medicare through work but no longer qualify under the new rules.
Social Security says that if someone later gets an eligible status, such as a green card, they can apply again.
And then there is the question every retiree asks next.
Does this touch Social Security retirement checks? According to the agency's own guidance, quoted by the Center for Medicare Advocacy: "These changes do not affect Social Security benefit eligibility or payment rules."
IBTimes reports the same. So a person who loses Medicare under this rule still keeps the monthly Social Security benefit they earned.
What they lose is the health coverage that came with it. And for people on dialysis or with serious illness, that is the part that matters most.
IBTimes notes that older people, people with disabilities and patients with end-stage kidney disease are among those potentially affected.
But how many people will actually lose coverage? The government's own numbers do not agree.
There are three different numbers in this story, and they measure different things.
First, about 300,000: the number of preliminary notices the federal rule expects to be mailed, according to the Center for Medicare Advocacy and Insurance Business.
Second, about 32,000: the number of people Medicare's own actuaries estimated would actually lose coverage, according to Insurance Business and IBTimes.
Third, 100,000: an estimate from the Congressional Budget Office in 2025 of how many people would lose coverage, cited by Insurance Business and the Center for Medicare Advocacy.
The Center notes that 300,000 letters is "significantly larger" than the budget office's projection.
In other words, far more people will get a scary letter than are expected to lose coverage.
And that is why how you answer matters.
According to Social Security guidance quoted by the Center for Medicare Advocacy and Insurance Business, people who get a notice can upload proof of their status through their online "my Social Security" account, using the "Upload Documents" option.
They can visit a local Social Security office. Or they can call Social Security using the phone number printed on the letter itself.
Be careful with anyone who calls you first: use the number on the official letter, not a number someone gives you on the phone.
The notices tell people to respond by the deadline printed in the letter; none of the reports we read gave a single date for everyone.
If Social Security then ends or denies Medicare, the person has 60 days from receiving that decision to file a written appeal, according to Insurance Business and IBTimes.
But IBTimes adds a hard limit: an appeal can fix a wrong decision, but it "cannot override the statutory immigration requirements."
Social Security says it is following the law. A spokesperson, quoted by Newsweek and cited by Insurance Business, described the effort as "maintaining the integrity of the program while protecting access to benefits for eligible beneficiaries."
IBTimes reports the agency says it is implementing legislation passed by Congress, and is responsible for making sure Medicare goes to eligible recipients.
The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, quoted by Insurance Business, said: "Patients losing Medicare coverage may explore health insurance options through their own employer or that of their spouse."
Social Security also points people to state health programs and low-cost community health centers, IBTimes says.
Supporters of the law see it as tightening eligibility for a program many Americans paid into for decades.
Critics see it very differently.

Tiffany Huyenh-Cho of the advocacy group Justice in Aging told Newsweek: "Terribly, the new law provides no exceptions."
Insurance Business reports she also warned that Social Security may not have accurate citizenship or immigration data, so some people who are still eligible could be sent notices by mistake.
The Center's own alert put it bluntly: "Thousands of lawfully present immigrants will be terminated from Medicare in early 2027."
The Center for Medicare Advocacy says many of those affected "are likely to end up uninsured," because the same law also restricts Medicaid and Affordable Care Act coverage for the same groups.
And according to Insurance Business and IBTimes, citing the Washington Post, the affected immigrants had paid more than 6 billion dollars into Medicare, based on federal estimates.
IBTimes notes those calculations have not been independently verified. None of the reports said whether any of those payments would be returned.
And private Medicare plans are part of this too.
If you are in a Medicare Advantage plan or a Part D drug plan and lose eligibility, the plan loses you too.
Insurance Business, citing the Center for Medicare Advocacy, reports that Medicare will disenroll those members automatically once Social Security updates its records, and the plans are not required to send their own notices.
That means some people may learn about the change only from the original Social Security letter.
If you help an older parent or a neighbor with their mail, this is one letter not to throw away.
And for people still working, there is another wrinkle.
Insurance Business explains that when Medicare ends, an employer group plan becomes the only payer.
Under federal rules, a group plan already pays first for workers 65 and older at employers with 20 or more employees, and for disabled workers at employers with 100 or more.
Federal HIPAA rules require a special enrollment period after losing group coverage or Medicaid, Insurance Business says, and tax rules allow, but do not require, plans to let workers change elections after losing Medicare, though Insurance Business notes that plan documents decide when election changes are allowed.
According to KFF figures cited by Insurance Business, 44 percent of likely Temporary Protected Status workers had employer coverage in 2024.
University of Pennsylvania researchers estimate about 70 percent of TPS holders are in the workforce.
So the hardest hit will be retirees with no job-based coverage at all.
The provision comes from H.R. 1, the law its supporters named the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed on July 4, 2025.
Insurance Business reports the same law sets 80-hour-a-month work requirements for many adults on Medicaid and food stamps, and notes that more than 71 million people rely on Medicaid.
The Medicare change was put into effect through a final rule from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services in early November; Insurance Business dates it November 4, and IBTimes says it was published November 9.
Social Security then began the mailings. Congress could change the law. At the time of this report, no change had been reported.
If you are a citizen or a green card holder and you get one of these letters, the guidance quoted by the Center for Medicare Advocacy says to send proof of your status by the deadline, online, in person or by phone.
That is exactly the risk Justice in Aging raised: records can be wrong.
Keep a copy of everything you send, and write down the date you sent it.
Do not ignore it, because the letter says coverage may end February 1 unless eligibility is established.
So: about 300,000 Medicare beneficiaries are getting preliminary letters. Refugees, asylees, TPS holders and some trafficking survivors lose eligibility on February 1, 2027.
Citizens and green card holders keep it. Social Security checks are not affected.
And the government expects about 32,000 people to actually lose coverage.
