The U.S. Navy says it will not rename the aircraft carrier USS Doris Miller.
CNN reported on November 20 that a spokesperson for Acting Navy Secretary Hung Cao told the network: "There is no intention to change the name of the USS Doris Miller."
USNI News, the naval news service, reported the same day that the Pearl Harbor hero will stay the namesake of the carrier, hull number CVN-81.
That ends three months of reports that the Navy was considering naming the ship for President Trump instead.
In the next minutes: how this started, what the family said, the Medal of Honor push, and what one mess attendant did on the morning America went to war.
The statement to CNN was short: "There is no intention to change the name of the USS Doris Miller." 9 words, after 3 months of silence.
CNN reports the Navy is no longer considering a new name. CNN did not report who made the final call, or why.
The ship is a Ford-class carrier, one of the biggest warships in the world.
It is still under construction. According to an earlier CNN report, its keel-laying ceremony was expected at the end of 2026.
And for one family in Texas, this news was a long time coming.
Thomas Bledsoe, Doris Miller's great-nephew, applauded the decision and called it a historic moment, CNN reports.
He said: "Doris Miller's story is not just our family's history; it is American history."
He added that Miller's courage "deserves to be remembered, honored, and recognized for generations to come."
For the family, it had been 3 hard months. In August, the Daily Beast reported, relatives went on television and said they had been blindsided by the reports.
To understand why, you need to know what CNN reported in August.
The Navy was working to rename the carrier, according to 3 sources familiar with internal discussions, CNN reported on August 21. 2 of those sources said there had been conversations about renaming it to honor President Trump.
CNN called a carrier named for a sitting president "unprecedented." CNN also reported that, inside the Navy, officials had stopped calling the ship USS Doris Miller and were using only its hull number, CVN-81.
A White House executive order on shipbuilding also referred to it only as CVN-81, CNN reported.
At the time, the Pentagon said it had "nothing to announce." The White House was never quoted as confirming a plan to put the President's name on the ship.
Then the family found out from the news.
In August, Bledsoe told CNN the family had not been informed of any name change.
Another relative, great-niece LaKisha Stansberry of Arlington, Texas, told Spectrum News she chose to believe it would not happen.
She said her great-uncle "gave his life for our country, so why would we change?"
She remembered a steel-cutting ceremony for the carrier as pure "euphoria." The White House referred questions to the Pentagon, Spectrum reported, and the Navy said it had no updates.
For 90 days, that is where things sat. But there was a second story inside the first one.
CNN reported in August that the Navy was recommending Doris Miller for the Medal of Honor, the nation's highest award for valor.
Final authorization rests with Congress, and requires the President's approval, CNN reported.
Bledsoe called the recommendation "very positive" and said the family had pushed for it for years.
Stansberry told Spectrum the family has fought for the medal too. CNN's November report repeated that the recommendation exists.
So the same Navy that was weighing taking his name off a ship was also asking to give him the country's top medal.
Whether that medal comes is the next question. And the debate over the name was part of a bigger fight.
CNN reported in August that Acting Secretary Cao's office was reviewing the Navy's naming rules, including whether presidents may be honored.
A renaming commission had been set up in 2025 under former Navy Secretary John Phelan, who was removed before it finished.
Phelan reportedly believed carriers should carry the names of presidents, admirals and great naval battles.
In 2025, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth also ordered the ship USNS Harvey Milk renamed.
A Pentagon spokesman, Sean Parnell, said then that names should reflect "the commander-in-chief's priorities, our nation's history, and the warrior ethos."
Supporters of the Miller name say he fits that last part exactly.
And the man who first put his name on the ship said the same thing.
The name was announced in January 2020, during President Trump's first term, CNN reports.
Then-acting Navy Secretary Thomas Modly unveiled it at a Martin Luther King Jr.
Day ceremony at Pearl Harbor. Modly said "one of our most powerful warships should bear the name of a sailor like Miller," and said the choice was recommended by retired Black admirals.
It was the first aircraft carrier ever named for an enlisted sailor, and the first named for an African American, CNN reports.
Spectrum News reports that the Navy at the time said the next carrier would bear "the proud name" of USS Doris Miller.
According to USNI News, as cited by CNN, the ship is not expected to be delivered until 2034.
A name that a Trump-era Navy chose has been kept by a Trump-era Navy.

So who was Doris Miller? He was the son of sharecroppers in Waco, Texas, Spectrum News reports.
He joined the Navy in 1939. On the morning of December 7, 1941, he was a mess attendant third class aboard the battleship USS West Virginia at Pearl Harbor, collecting laundry when the bombs began to fall.
Mess attendant was one of the few jobs the Navy then allowed Black sailors to do, CNN reports.
Miller helped carry his wounded commanding officer. Then he took over an anti-aircraft machine gun he had never been trained to use.
The Department of Veterans Affairs record says that "although untrained," he "laid down effective fire," and stopped firing only when he ran out of ammunition.
Think about what that means. A young man the Navy would only let serve food and wash laundry turned out to be one of the men who fought back hardest that morning.
Historian Kurt Piehler of Florida State University told Spectrum News: "we wasted a lot of talent in World War II because of segregation and discrimination."
He said Miller's service was "above and beyond the call of duty."
Many men watching this channel grew up hearing the stories of Pearl Harbor from fathers and uncles who were there, or who enlisted the next week.
The 2,400 Americans killed that morning are remembered every December. Miller's story was nearly left out.
It took a fight just to get him recognized.
In 1942, Admiral Chester Nimitz, commander of the Pacific Fleet, pinned the Navy Cross on Doris Miller.
Spectrum News reports he was the first African American to receive it.
He then went on a war bond tour, the VA says, as the first African American allowed on that tour.
And then he went back to sea. About a year later, in 1943, during the Battle of Makin in the Pacific, his ship was hit by a Japanese submarine's torpedo, CNN reports, and Miller was killed.
Hollywood remembered him decades later: Cuba Gooding Jr. played him in the 2001 film "Pearl Harbor."
The Navy remembered him with a ship before, and again with one of its largest.
Why does a name on a ship matter so much? Because a carrier carries it for 50 years or more, around the world, with 5,000 sailors aboard who learn the story behind it.
The Navy has named carriers for presidents, admirals and battles. This one was the first named for an enlisted man, and the first for an African American, CNN reports.
That is why, for 3 months, the family heard the Navy's own people calling it only CVN-81, and feared what came next.
For many veterans, the question was never about one president. It was about whether a sailor who did his job with no training, no rank and no glory could hold a place next to the admirals.
On November 20, the Navy's answer was yes. The decision did not come with a long statement or a ceremony.
Just 9 words to a reporter. But for a family in Texas, those words carried the weight of a Medal of Honor citation they are still waiting for.
So here is where it stands. The Navy says it has no intention of renaming the USS Doris Miller, ending three months of reports it might name the carrier for President Trump.
The family calls it a historic moment.
3 things to watch. First, the keel-laying ceremony for CVN-81, which CNN reported was expected around the end of 2026.
Second, the Medal of Honor: the Navy's recommendation needs Congress and the President.
Third, the naming review in Cao's office, which could still change the rules for future carriers.
Pearl Harbor's anniversary is December 7, 2026. On that anniversary, the family of one of its heroes would mark it knowing his name stays on the ship.
Where it stands: the name stays, and a Pearl Harbor sailor keeps his place on a carrier.
