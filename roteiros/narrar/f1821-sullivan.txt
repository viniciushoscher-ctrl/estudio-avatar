Alaska has voted out Republican Senator Dan Sullivan. Democrat Mary Peltola beat the two-term senator after Alaska counted its ranked-choice ballots, and CBS News and the Associated Press reported her win on November 17, 2026.
A preliminary ranked-choice tally by FairVote has Peltola ahead by 10,622 votes.
Alaska was the last Senate race of the midterms. With it, Democrats hold 56 seats to the Republicans' 44.
Sullivan, a retired Marine colonel, has not conceded. And the fight over a second candidate named Dan Sullivan is still in federal court.
In the next minutes: what Sullivan loses on January 3, the Justice Department case that could still be appealed, and how it ended for the last two Alaska senators the voters sent home.
The race was called that night. CBS News projected Peltola the winner, and the AP reported her victory in a story by Becky Bohrer.
Peltola spoke that night. "Thank you Alaska. Let's get to work," she said in a statement, pledging to "take on corporate greed, lower costs, and put Alaska first."
She told voters they had sent a message that "the status quo in D.C. is not working for Alaska," the AP reports.
Sullivan did not concede. His campaign said it would let the process "run its course until every legal vote is counted and certified."
His spokesperson did not answer the AP's request for comment. So the winner has spoken, and the loser has not.
But the numbers behind the call show why the networks did not wait.
On first-choice votes, Peltola had 49.95 percent and Sullivan 45.91 percent, according to FairVote's preliminary tabulation.
That left her just short of a majority, so Alaska's ranked-choice system kicked in.
Voters for the candidates at the bottom had their next choices counted.
In the final round, Peltola had 51.9 percent and Sullivan 48.1 percent.
The margin was 10,622 votes. The count included 285,439 ballots, about 98 percent of the total, FairVote says, with fewer than 6,000 left.
Even if every remaining ballot went to Sullivan, he would still fall short.
That is why the call came before the last ballots were counted.
FairVote estimates total turnout at roughly 291,000 ballots, in a state where a few thousand votes have decided Senate races before.
But one name on that ballot is the reason this race may end up in court, and it is a name you already know.
There were two Dan Sullivans on the ballot. The second is Daniel J.
Sullivan, a retired teacher who entered the race in June. Alaska's Division of Elections first ruled him ineligible, saying his filing was not made in good faith.
A state judge reversed that, and the Alaska Supreme Court agreed, so his name stayed on.
The New York Times reported allegations that Peltola's team helped recruit him.
Peltola denied it. Daniel J. Sullivan has denied any contact with her campaign and says the run was his own choice.
The senator called him a "sham candidate." In the primary, the second Sullivan got about 2.5 percent.
In the final count, FairVote found 4,684 ballots ranked him first. More of those went to Peltola than to the senator.
That fight did not end at the ballot box, and the Justice Department got involved.
About a week and a half before Election Day, the Trump administration sued to block certification of the race if Sullivan lost because of the second Sullivan, CBS reports.
A federal judge dismissed that lawsuit, the AP reports, and the Justice Department has appealed.
The department has also confirmed a criminal investigation, but has not said what crimes it is examining.
President Trump called for Peltola's prosecution and mentioned the second Sullivan at an October rally, according to the AP.
Nobody has been charged. Peltola denies wrongdoing. And CBS reports that Peltola's margin was large enough that the second Sullivan's votes would not have changed the result.
The appeal is still pending. So what does Sullivan lose if the courts leave the result alone?
He loses the seat when the new Senate is sworn in on January 3.
He loses his post on the Armed Services Committee, where he chairs the subcommittee on readiness and management support, and sits on the airland and seapower panels.
He loses the majority's agenda too, because his party will be in the minority, 44 seats to 56.
He keeps his rank and his service record. Sullivan served on active duty as a Marine and retired from the Reserve as a colonel, Wikipedia records.
He also has campaign money. As of late July, FEC filings showed Sullivan had raised about 11.7 million dollars and had about 7.3 million in cash.
Peltola had raised about 18.5 million. He was outraised by about 6.8 million dollars by that point, and outside groups spent heavily during the primary alone.
Right for Alaska spent about 1.2 million dollars, and Fair Deal Alaska about 770,000, according to Wikipedia's summary of FEC data.
And his loss is only one of nine.
Democrats flipped nine Republican seats in these midterms, the AP reports. Alaska was the last race to be decided.
Democrats also won in South Carolina, Texas, Kansas and Mississippi. The new Senate will have 56 Democrats and 44 Republicans.
In Alaska, Democrat Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins also won the governor's office, the first Democrat elected governor there in decades, the AP reports.
Alaska's only House seat, between Republican Nick Begich and independent Bill Hill, still had not been called.
Republicans sent big names north to save Sullivan, including Vice President JD Vance, CBS reports.
The GOP's main super PAC, the Senate Leadership Fund, spent heavily. It was not enough.
And Alaskans who remember the last time they sent a senator home know how close those endings can be.

In 2008, Alaska voted out Ted Stevens, the longest-serving Republican senator in history at the time.
Stevens had served since December 1968, about 40 years. In July 2008, he was indicted on seven felony counts of failing to properly report gifts, including home renovations and gifts tied to an oil-services company worth more than 250,000 dollars.
A jury convicted him on October 27, 2008. Eight days later, on November 4, Democrat Mark Begich beat him by 3,724 votes.
Then the Justice Department found gross prosecutorial misconduct, and Attorney General Eric Holder asked the court to throw out the conviction.
The judge did. Stevens was cleared, but his seat was gone. That race was decided by 1.3 percent.
Sullivan's was decided by more, and the reasons are different.
Polls showed a dead heat for months. A New York Times/Siena poll from late September had Peltola at 50 and Sullivan at 43.
Trafalgar had Sullivan ahead by one point. The polling averages in early October, as compiled on Wikipedia, had Peltola up by about 2.4 points, 48.5 to 46.1.
The final first-choice result, about 4 points, was wider than almost every average predicted.
CBS reports that the unpopular war in Iran and high gas and diesel prices helped Peltola.
In a state where diesel heats homes and runs villages, that matters.
Peltola ran on cost of living. Her statement promised to "lower costs."
Sullivan ran as a Marine and a defense hawk, with President Trump's endorsement.
In the end, the voters who ranked a third candidate first split more toward Peltola.
And the man Sullivan beat to win this seat in the first place knows exactly how that feels.
Mark Begich, the Democrat who beat Ted Stevens in 2008, served one term.
In 2014, Dan Sullivan, then a former Alaska attorney general, took the seat from him, 47.96 percent to 45.83 percent.
It was a Republican wave year, and Begich became the incumbent who lost.
Twelve years later, the seat has turned again, this time against Sullivan.
Peltola will be only the fifth Democrat since statehood to represent Alaska in the Senate, the AP reports.
Begich was the last one. Sullivan won the seat from a Democrat and loses it to one.
And the woman taking it has already made history once.
Mary Peltola is 53 and Yup'ik. She was the first Alaska Native to serve in Congress, the AP reports, when she won Alaska's House seat, the first Democrat to hold it in 50 years, according to CBS.
She lost that seat two years ago to Nick Begich, by under 3 points, while Trump carried Alaska by 13.
She becomes the first Alaska Native elected to the U.S. Senate. And the first Democrat elected to the Senate from Alaska since 2008, CBS reports.
She will join a 56-seat majority. That gives Democrats control of committees, nominations and the Senate calendar.
It also means Sullivan's old Armed Services seat goes to someone new.
Before any of that, the count still has to be certified.
So here is where it stands. Peltola leads Sullivan 51.9 to 48.1 percent after ranked choice, by 10,622 votes.
