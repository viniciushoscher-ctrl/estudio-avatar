The Justice Department says it has charged 15 people across the country with stealing from America's veterans, and some of them worked inside the VA.
On November 17, 2026, federal prosecutors and the VA's inspector general announced the first nationwide crackdown on veterans-benefits fraud, Military.com and FOX 5 New York report.
The newly charged cases add up to more than 3.4 million dollars in alleged losses.
Convictions, guilty pleas and sentences announced this fall add up to more than 19 million dollars.
In the next minutes: the call-center worker accused of rerouting disability payments, the Ohio money manager accused of draining 940,000 dollars, the fake veterans, and how it ended the last time the man at the top was the thief.
The operation started on September 1, Military.com reports. The Justice Department's Fraud Division and 17 U.S.
Attorney's offices worked together with the VA's Office of Inspector General. It was the first coordinated national surge on VA benefits fraud.
The result: 15 people charged, with alleged intended losses of more than 3.4 million dollars.
In the same period, sentences, guilty pleas and trial verdicts in older cases totaled more than 19 million dollars in intended losses.
The announcement came at a press conference during National Veterans Awareness Week.
Every person newly charged is presumed innocent unless proven guilty in court.
But some of the accusations hit close to home for every veteran watching.
In West Texas, a VA call-center employee has been indicted, accused of changing veterans' direct-deposit information so that their disability payments went into her own accounts, FOX 5 reports.
Think about what that means. A veteran calls the VA for help.
The person on the other end has access to where the money goes.
If the accusation is proven, the veteran's check simply stops arriving, and the money goes somewhere else.
She is one of the cases brought by 17 U.S. Attorney's offices since September 1.
She has not been convicted, and she is presumed innocent. But prosecutors say the case shows why they are looking inside the system, not just outside it.
And it was not the only case involving a VA insider.
In Houston, a 61-year-old former prosthetics representative at the Houston VA Medical Center has been charged with conspiracy to commit wire fraud, Military.com reports.
Prosecutors allege she steered purchase orders to one VA-approved vendor and approved inflated delivery fees, in what FOX 5 describes as a kickback scheme.
The government says the VA lost about 182,000 dollars. According to Military.com, the alleged conduct covered roughly 1,437 transactions over almost five years, ending in August of last year, mostly for wheelchairs and walkers.
The government is seeking to recover at least 182,000 dollars. She is charged, not convicted.
And the largest alleged theft in the new cases did not happen inside the VA at all.
In Ohio, a woman who served as the financial manager for severely disabled veterans has been indicted, FOX 5 reports.
She is accused of draining more than 940,000 dollars from their accounts.
When a veteran cannot manage his own money, the VA can appoint someone to do it, called a fiduciary.
That person controls the benefits. If the accusation is true, the people trusted to protect the most vulnerable veterans took their money instead.
She has not been convicted. In Washington, D.C., another man is accused of using the identity of his disabled 78-year-old neighbor, a veteran, to take VA and Social Security money, both outlets report.
According to Military.com, he is also accused of using a dead veteran's identity to collect about 70,000 dollars in VA benefits, and of opening credit cards in his neighbor's name to fund his own business.
He has been indicted, not convicted. Prosecutors say these schemes are growing.
The Federal Trade Commission reported that military members, veterans and their families lost 684 million dollars to fraud in 2025, up 17 percent from 584 million dollars the year before, according to Military.com.
That figure covers all kinds of scams, not only VA benefits. The Justice Department created its National Fraud Enforcement Division in April, and Military.com reports it is part of President Trump's Task Force to Eliminate Fraud.
Assistant Attorney General Colin McDonald leads it. "For veterans, benefits are not simply government payments," he said, according to FOX 5, stressing that they are earned through service.
So how do these schemes work?
Put the cases side by side and you see five patterns. One: someone with access changes where your payment goes.
Two: someone who buys equipment for veterans inflates the bill. Three: someone appointed to manage a veteran's money takes it.
Four: someone steals a veteran's identity, sometimes a dead veteran's. Five: someone pretends to be a veteran, or pretends to be a veteran-owned business, to get money meant for real ones.
All five showed up in the cases announced on November 17, according to Military.com and FOX 5.
The common thread is trust. Most of these schemes do not need a hacker.
They need a person the system already trusts. That is why prosecutors are looking at insiders as closely as outsiders.
So who has already been convicted?
Some cases are already finished. In Florida, a jury convicted two men, Matthew West and Kevin Kutina, over an 8.9 million dollar contract that was meant for businesses owned by service-disabled veterans, FOX 5 reports.
The contract was for a cancer infusion therapy center at the Bay Pines VA.
The center was only partly built, and it had to be torn down.
Another Florida jury convicted two men, Zachary Hiscock and Arif Sayed, in a scheme using illegal commission-based recruiting to enroll veterans in IT and cybersecurity programs paid by the GI Bill, FOX 5 reports.
Christina Nolte stole 240,000 dollars from the VA and used it to pay off her student loans; she got one year in prison, Military.com reports.

Michael Duane Dyson of Tallahassee, Florida, falsely claimed to be a U.S.
Army combat veteran, Military.com reports. Using that claim, he received more than 114,000 dollars in VA-funded medical treatment.
He was sentenced in July to four years and ordered to pay about 28,000 dollars in restitution.
For the men who really served, cases like this sting twice: once for the money, and once for the honor.
The VA's inspector general, Cheryl Mason, said "training and awareness" can help VA employees recognize and report fraud.
But the worst VA scandal in history started at the very top.
In 1921, President Warren Harding put his friend Charles Forbes in charge of the new Veterans Bureau, created to care for the men coming home from World War One.
Forbes was later accused of selling off government hospital supplies at a fraction of their value and of taking kickbacks on hospital construction contracts.
In 1925, a federal jury convicted him of conspiracy to defraud the government.
He was sentenced to two years in Leavenworth federal prison, according to historical accounts.
The bureau he ran was later folded into the Veterans Administration in 1930.
The lesson veterans learned a century ago still holds: the system is only as honest as the people running it.
Which brings us to Texas.
At the press conference, four U.S. Attorneys from Texas announced a new Texas Federal Fraud Task Force, combining federal, state and local law enforcement against regional fraud rings, both outlets report.
They are Aaron Reitz of the Southern District, Justin Simmons of the Western District, Ryan Raybould of the Northern District and Jay Combs of the Eastern District.
Reitz said the fraud harms both veterans and taxpayers. Combs, who is a veteran himself, said of fraud against veterans: "It will not be tolerated in the Eastern District of Texas," FOX 5 reports.
Texas has one of the largest veteran populations in the country. The next big VA scandal Americans remember was about something else: waiting.
In 2014, whistleblowers revealed that the Phoenix VA hospital had kept secret waiting lists to hide how long veterans waited for care.
An inspector general's review found widespread problems with scheduling, and reports linked delays to veterans' deaths.
The scandal spread nationwide. On May 30, 2014, VA Secretary Eric Shinseki, a retired four-star Army general, resigned.
Congress passed a law that summer allowing more veterans to see private doctors.
Phoenix was not about stealing money. But it taught the same lesson as Forbes: when veterans are hurt, the pressure only comes when the public pays attention.
So here is where it stands. Fifteen people charged nationwide, with more than 3.4 million dollars in alleged losses.
Over 19 million dollars in finished cases this fall. A new task force in Texas.
Everyone newly charged is presumed innocent.
The 15 defendants go through the courts, where each will have the chance to answer the charges.
The surge ran from early September to mid-November across 17 U.S. Attorney's offices, FOX 5 reports, and the new Texas task force will go after regional fraud rings.
